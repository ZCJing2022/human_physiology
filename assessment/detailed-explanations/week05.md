## w05-mc01

The important distinction is the **direction of information flow relative to the central nervous system (CNS)**, which consists of the brain and spinal cord. Afferent pathways bring information toward the CNS, so **B is correct**. For example, pressure on the skin activates sensory receptors, and sensory axons carry information about that pressure into the spinal cord and onward through central pathways.

Option A describes the opposite direction: an efferent or motor command travels from the CNS toward an effector such as skeletal muscle. Option C describes communication within the CNS rather than the sensory input division of the peripheral nervous system. Option D describes endocrine signalling, in which hormones travel through the blood rather than along a sensory nerve.

## w05-mc02

**Proprioception tells the nervous system where the body and its parts are and how they are moving**, making B the correct answer. You can usually tell whether your elbow is bent even with your eyes closed because receptors associated with muscles, tendons, and joints provide information about muscle length, tension, and joint configuration.

This information has both conscious and unconscious uses. It contributes to your awareness of limb position, but it also helps spinal and brain circuits adjust posture and coordinate movement without requiring you to think about every muscle.

Blood glucose is a chemical variable monitored for metabolic regulation, not body position. Sound frequency is a property handled by the auditory system. Visceral pain signals potential problems in internal organs rather than limb position.

## w05-mc03

The sequence in **B** follows the causal order of sensory processing. First, a stimulus such as pressure, light, or a dissolved chemical reaches an appropriate receptor. The receptor **transduces** that stimulus into a change in membrane potential. Information is then transmitted along an afferent pathway toward the CNS, where neurons process it and may use it for perception or a reflex response.

In some systems the receptor is the sensory ending of the afferent neuron itself. In others, a separate receptor cell releases transmitter onto the afferent neuron.

Option A reverses the direction of sensory input. Option C starts with a motor pathway, which carries output rather than sensory information. An endocrine gland is not a required intermediate in the common sensory pathway, so D does not describe the general organization being tested.

## w05-mc04

Pressure and vibration deform tissue, so they are **mechanical stimuli**. A receptor that converts this deformation into an electrical response is a **mechanoreceptor**, making C correct. In a cutaneous receptor, deformation can change the opening of mechanically sensitive ion channels and thereby alter the receptor's membrane potential.

Photoreceptors respond most effectively to light; chemoreceptors respond to chemical substances; thermoreceptors respond to temperature changes. These labels concern the stimulus that initiates transduction, not the eventual form of the neural signal. All of these systems can ultimately communicate with the CNS through electrical activity.

This principle also explains why auditory hair cells are mechanoreceptors even though the resulting perception is hearing rather than touch.

## w05-mc05

A **receptive field** is the region in which a stimulus can influence the activity of a particular sensory neuron. For a touch-sensitive afferent, it is the patch of skin monitored by that neuron's sensory endings. **B is therefore correct**: the definition connects a spatial region with a particular neuron, rather than referring to the entire sensory cortex.

If one neuron monitors a very large patch, its firing alone provides relatively imprecise information about where within that patch a stimulus occurred. Smaller fields, especially when combined with dense innervation, allow neighboring locations to activate more distinguishable patterns.

Option C describes a threshold, which concerns how strong a stimulus must be, not where it acts. A motor unit in D consists of a motor neuron and its muscle fibers and belongs to motor control.

## w05-mc06

**Transduction is the conversion from stimulus energy into an electrical response in a receptor**, so A is correct. For example, bending a hair-cell bundle changes ion-channel opening, while light activates a photopigment and a signalling cascade.

That first response is usually a **graded receptor potential**, not automatically an action potential. Depending on the receptor, it changes action-potential generation in the same sensory neuron or changes transmitter release onto a separate afferent neuron. Transduction should therefore be distinguished from the later propagation of action potentials along an axon.

Moving transmitter into a vesicle concerns transmitter storage, not the conversion of sensory energy. Muscle force is a motor output, and movement across the blood–brain barrier is a transport process. Neither defines sensory transduction.

## w05-mc07

The **adequate stimulus** is the form of energy to which a receptor is most sensitive, making B correct. For example, a photoreceptor is specialized to respond to light, whereas a cutaneous mechanoreceptor responds efficiently to mechanical deformation.

This distinction matters because sufficiently intense, nonpreferred stimulation can sometimes activate a sensory pathway. Mechanical stimulation of the eye, for instance, can produce a visual sensation without making pressure the adequate stimulus of photoreceptors.

Option A confuses receptor specialization with stimulus magnitude. Pain in C is a sensory experience associated with potentially damaging stimulation, not the definition of an adequate stimulus for every receptor. Arrival time at the cortex in D depends on pathway properties and does not determine receptor classification.

## w05-mc08

A receptor potential is **graded**: its magnitude can vary. **B is correct.** Within a receptor's operating range, changing the stimulus can change the size of its membrane-potential response.

If the receptor is part of an afferent neuron, a larger depolarizing receptor potential can drive more frequent action potentials at the spike-generating region. If the receptor is a separate cell, the voltage change can alter transmitter release onto the afferent neuron. Some receptor responses, notably those of photoreceptors to light, are hyperpolarizing rather than depolarizing.

Option A describes the all-or-none property of action potentials, not receptor potentials. Receptor potentials are sensory responses, so they are not restricted to motor neurons. An ECG is a recording of summed cardiac electrical activity and is a different kind of measurement altogether.

## w05-mc09

**Lateral inhibition sharpens the contrast between neighboring sensory pathways**, which is why B is correct. A stimulus often activates several overlapping receptive fields, but the pathway nearest the stimulus center may be activated most strongly. Through inhibitory circuits, strong activity suppresses neighboring pathways and makes the central peak stand out more clearly.

The CNS can use that contrast to estimate location or detect an edge more precisely.

Option A conflicts with the all-or-none nature of action potentials. Enlarging receptive fields, as in C, generally reduces spatial precision rather than explaining contrast enhancement. Option D is too absolute: lateral inhibition does not simply switch off every weak stimulus throughout the nervous system. It changes neighboring responses in a spatially organized manner.

## w05-mc10

A stronger stimulus commonly produces a larger receptor potential, which can drive **more action potentials per unit time** in the sensory axon. Thus **B is correct**. The individual spikes remain approximately the same size; their timing changes.

Across a population, stronger stimuli may also recruit additional receptors and afferent fibers. Frequency coding within active axons and population coding across axons can therefore work together to represent intensity.

Option A confuses the graded amplitude of a receptor potential with the all-or-none amplitude of an action potential. Reversing polarity or slowing conduction is not the normal intensity code. Conduction velocity depends mainly on properties such as axon diameter and myelination, rather than being deliberately reduced to signal a stronger stimulus.

## w05-mc11

A **slowly adapting, or tonic, receptor** continues to signal while a stimulus remains present, so B is the best answer. Its firing may decline somewhat from the initial response, but enough activity persists to provide information about the sustained condition. This is useful when the nervous system needs to monitor continued pressure, muscle length, or body position.

A rapidly adapting, or phasic, receptor emphasizes changes. It may respond strongly when a stimulus begins and then markedly reduce its activity even though the stimulus continues. That makes it useful for detecting transitions but less suitable as the sole reporter of an unchanging stimulus.

Photoreceptors alone do not define the general category requested, and a silent receptor would not reliably report a persistent stimulus.

## w05-mc12

**A is correct because fast, sharp pain is carried mainly by small myelinated Aδ afferents.** Myelin allows action potentials to propagate rapidly between nodes of Ranvier, so these fibers convey a relatively early warning after a potentially damaging stimulus.

Slow burning or aching pain is associated more strongly with small unmyelinated C fibers. Their signals arrive later because conduction is slower. A single injury can activate both populations, helping explain why an immediate sharp sensation may be followed by a lingering ache.

Large motor axons in B carry commands to muscles rather than pain information toward the CNS. Unmyelinated autonomic efferents in C are also output pathways, not the sensory afferents that carry pain. Retinal ganglion cells in D belong to vision.

## w05-mc13

**Convergence of visceral and somatic afferent information on shared central neurons** provides the key explanation for referred pain, so A is correct. A central neuron may receive input both from an internal organ and from a region of the body surface. Activity in that central pathway therefore does not identify its peripheral source perfectly.

Because the CNS often associates that pathway with somatic input, visceral activity can be interpreted as originating from a familiar surface region. This is an error of source attribution in central processing, not evidence that the pain-producing disturbance has physically moved to the skin.

Larger action potentials in B are not the normal code for either pain intensity or location. Retinal convergence concerns vision, and cochlear lateral inhibition concerns auditory processing.

## w05-mc14

The five basic taste qualities emphasized in this course are **sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami**, so A is correct. These are categories of gustatory information detected by taste receptor cells. Umami is associated with certain amino acids, including glutamate, rather than being another name for sweetness or smell.

A food's overall **flavor** is broader than taste alone. It combines gustatory signals with smell, temperature, texture, and chemical irritation. This is why the other lists may contain familiar eating experiences without listing the five basic tastes. The burning quality described as spicy is mainly a somatosensory response, while cooling sensations and pressure also involve nongustatory mechanisms.

## w05-mc15

**A is correct:** an odorant receptor commonly activates the G protein Golf, leading to increased cyclic AMP, or cAMP. Odorant molecules dissolve in the mucus over the olfactory epithelium and bind receptors on olfactory cilia. The activated receptor stimulates Golf, which activates adenylyl cyclase; that enzyme converts ATP into cAMP.

The increase in cAMP opens cyclic-nucleotide-gated cation channels. Inward positive current depolarizes the olfactory receptor neuron, and sufficient depolarization can increase action-potential firing.

Option B skips the receptor and second-messenger steps: odorants do not normally open voltage-gated sodium channels by binding directly to them. Option C gives the wrong messenger direction and receptor system. Skeletal muscle contraction in D is not the primary transduction event.

## w05-mc16

**B identifies the unusual organization of olfaction.** Olfactory receptor neurons project to the olfactory bulb, and output from the bulb reaches primary olfactory cortical regions without first requiring the obligatory thalamic relay familiar from many other sensory pathways.

The wording does not mean that the thalamus has no role anywhere in smell-related processing. Later pathways involved in more elaborate identification and conscious evaluation can involve thalamic connections.

Olfactory input does not first need to enter the spinal cord, so A is wrong. It certainly reaches the forebrain, contradicting C. The optic nerve in D carries retinal information, not smell. A reliable sequence is receptor neuron, olfactory nerve fibers, olfactory bulb, olfactory tract, and olfactory cortical targets.

## w05-mc17

Sweet, bitter, and umami begin with **G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs)**, making A correct. Binding of a tastant activates a receptor-linked signalling cascade inside the taste cell. Intracellular signalling then changes ion handling and membrane potential, ultimately enabling the cell to communicate with gustatory afferents.

The receptor initiates intracellular events rather than acting as a voltage-gated sodium channel. In the course's comparison, salty and sour transduction depend more directly on ion-mediated mechanisms. The two strategies differ at the input stage but converge on the functional outcome of signalling the presence of a tastant.

Voltage-gated sodium channels respond to membrane voltage, so B does not identify these taste receptors. Stretch receptors respond to mechanical deformation, and photopigments participate in light detection.

## w05-mc18

**Hertz, abbreviated Hz, means cycles per second**, so B is correct. A frequency of 500 Hz means that the pressure pattern repeats 500 times each second. Frequency is the physical property most directly associated with the perceptual distinction between lower and higher pitch.

Decibels in A express a sound level on a logarithmic scale; they do not count wave cycles. Millivolts in C measure electrical potential differences and could describe a membrane potential, not the frequency of an airborne wave. Liters per minute in D measure volume flow, such as cardiac output.

Sound-wave frequency and an auditory neuron's firing frequency are not interchangeable measurements: the cochlea and neural circuits encode the sound, and a neuron does not necessarily fire one action potential for every cycle.

## w05-mc19

Wave **amplitude** concerns the size of the pressure variation, whereas **frequency** concerns how often that variation repeats. Increasing amplitude at otherwise comparable conditions increases sound intensity and usually perceived loudness, so B is the best answer.

For a sound travelling through the same medium, intensity increases with the square of the pressure amplitude. This is why turning up the volume of a tone can make it louder while leaving its pitch essentially unchanged.

Option A would be more directly associated with changing frequency. Wavelength in C depends on wave speed and frequency, not simply on increasing amplitude. Color in D belongs to visual perception. Loudness is a perceptual quantity and also depends on factors such as frequency and the listener, so amplitude and loudness are not identical concepts.

## w05-mc20

The **organ of Corti**, located in the cochlea, contains the hair cells responsible for auditory transduction, so B is correct. Mechanical movement in the cochlea bends their stereocilia, changes receptor-cell membrane potential, and alters transmitter release onto auditory afferents.

The tympanic membrane in C is essential, but its role is mechanical: it vibrates in response to sound and transfers motion to the ossicles. It is not the location of the auditory receptor cells. The auditory cortex in D interprets ascending neural information rather than receiving airborne sound directly.

Semicircular canals in A also contain hair-cell-based receptors, but they belong to the vestibular system and detect angular acceleration of the head. For hearing, choose the **cochlear organ of Corti**, not any structure that happens to contain hair cells.

## w05-mc21

**A is correct because hair-bundle movement changes the opening of mechanically gated ion channels.** When stereocilia bend in the excitatory direction, tension on tip links increases and transduction channels open. Positive ions, predominantly K⁺ from the potassium-rich endolymph, enter the hair cell and depolarize it.

Depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels near the cell's basal synapse. Calcium entry increases transmitter release onto auditory afferents, which can then fire more frequently. Bending in the opposite direction closes more transduction channels, hyperpolarizes the receptor, and reduces release.

A muscle action potential in B is not the receptor event. Rhodopsin in C belongs to vision, and erythropoietin in D regulates red-cell production. The hair cell itself is not simply generating larger auditory nerve action potentials.

## w05-mc22

**B is correct.** The narrower, stiffer region near the cochlear base responds best to higher frequencies, whereas the wider, more flexible region toward the apex responds best to lower frequencies.

Hair cells and their associated afferents preserve this spatial organization. The CNS can therefore use the identity of the active cochlear region as information about sound frequency. This is called **place coding** or tonotopic organization. Timing information can also contribute, particularly for lower frequencies, but place coding is the distinction this question targets.

Action-potential amplitude in A is not the normal pitch code: an individual spike does not become a different size to indicate a different note. Red-blood-cell counts and AV nodal delay concern other physiological systems.

## w05-mc23

A louder sound usually causes larger mechanical movement and stronger hair-cell responses. This increases transmitter release to auditory afferents and can produce **higher firing rates and recruitment of additional fibers**, making B correct.

This does not require an individual action potential to become larger. Once generated, a spike is essentially all-or-none. The nervous system instead changes how often spikes occur and how many afferents contribute. At high intensities, some fibers may approach their firing limits, so population activity is especially important rather than relying on a single axon indefinitely.

Option A confuses receptor-potential amplitude with action-potential amplitude. Stopping all firing in C would not be the typical response to a louder sound, and reversing conduction direction in D would disrupt rather than encode the normal sensory pathway.

## w05-mc24

Auditory information leaves the cochlea through the **cochlear division of cranial nerve VIII, the vestibulocochlear nerve**, making A correct. Hair cells release transmitter onto the peripheral endings of auditory afferent neurons. Those neurons carry action potentials centrally to the cochlear nuclei in the brainstem.

The hair cell produces a graded receptor potential and synaptic output; the auditory afferent supplies the action potentials that travel in the nerve.

The optic nerve in B carries visual information from retinal ganglion cells. Vagal motor axons in C carry autonomic output rather than cochlear sensory input. The corticospinal tract in D is a descending motor pathway. The word **vestibulocochlear** includes both balance and hearing components, but the cochlear component is the one that conveys the auditory signals specified here.

## w05-mc25

Two-point discrimination asks whether two nearby contacts can be perceived as separate rather than as one. **Small receptive fields and dense sensory innervation**, as in B, help because neighboring points are more likely to produce distinguishable patterns in separate afferent pathways.

Large receptive fields and extensive convergence, as in A, pool information from wider regions. That pooling can make it harder to identify which precise locations were stimulated. Lateral inhibition helps sharpen contrast between neighboring responses, so removing it in C would not explain excellent spatial discrimination.

C fibers in D are associated with particular sensory functions, including slow pain, rather than being a general basis for fine touch discrimination. Also distinguish receptor density from field size: they often work together, but they are not the same property.

## w05-mc26

**B describes labeled-line coding:** the CNS interprets a signal partly according to the receptor and neural pathway carrying it. Action potentials in different sensory axons are broadly similar electrical events. Their meaning depends on their origin, connections, and destination rather than on each sensory modality having a chemically unique kind of spike.

For example, activity in a visual pathway is processed as visual information, whereas activity in an auditory pathway is processed as auditory information.

Option A incorrectly suggests that spike chemistry itself labels the sensation. Option C is doubly misleading: many sensory afferents generate action potentials, and ordinary rods and cones primarily use graded potentials rather than sending optic-nerve spikes themselves. Option D gives resting voltage a role it cannot provide by itself.

## w05-mc27

A **rapidly adapting, or phasic, receptor** responds especially strongly when a stimulus changes, making B correct. When a pressure stimulus first appears, the receptor may fire rapidly; during an unchanging indentation, its response declines. Some such receptors also respond when the stimulus ends, depending on their mechanical and cellular properties.

That response pattern is useful for detecting movement, vibration, contact onset, or other transitions. Adaptation therefore changes which features of the stimulus are emphasized rather than indicating that sensory function has simply failed.

A slowly adapting receptor is more suitable for maintaining information about unchanging posture, so A is not the best match. Blood type is not detected by this kind of sensory receptor. Cardiac output in D is a cardiovascular variable, not the defining stimulus property of phasic receptors.

## w05-mc28

In the terminology used here, **sensation begins with detecting and encoding a stimulus, while perception is its conscious interpretation by the CNS**. B therefore gives the most useful distinction.

Not every sensory signal becomes a conscious perception. Proprioceptive input, for example, can help adjust movement through neural circuits without requiring deliberate awareness of each adjustment. Conversely, attention, prior experience, and the pattern of central processing can influence how sensory input is perceived.

Option A erases the distinction being tested. Option C incorrectly locates conscious interpretation in the peripheral receptor itself. Language is not required for receptors to detect a stimulus or for the nervous system to respond, so D is also wrong. A helpful organizing sequence is **detection, transduction, neural processing, and possible conscious perception**.

## w05-sr01

Begin with a stimulus, such as skin deformation, reaching a receptor that is sensitive to that form of energy. The receptor converts the stimulus into a graded change in membrane potential: this is transduction, and the electrical change is the receptor potential.

If the receptor is the ending of the primary afferent neuron, its graded response can influence action-potential generation in that neuron. If the receptor is a separate cell, it changes transmitter release onto a primary afferent, which then generates the travelling spikes. In either arrangement, the afferent pathway carries information toward the brain or spinal cord.

Central neurons receive and transform the signal through synaptic processing. The identity of the pathway helps specify modality, the pattern of active receptive fields helps specify location, and firing rate and recruitment help specify intensity. Central processing may produce conscious perception, an unconscious adjustment, or a reflex response.

For example, touching an object can both make you aware of contact and help adjust your grip.

## w05-sr02

Start by imagining two nearby points pressing on the skin. If the points primarily activate distinct, small receptive fields, the resulting activity patterns can remain sufficiently different for the CNS to represent two locations. Dense innervation increases the number of independent sensory channels available over a given area and therefore supports finer spatial sampling.

If receptive fields are large or many inputs converge strongly onto the same downstream neuron, two close contacts may produce a pooled response that is difficult to distinguish from one contact.

Lateral inhibition adds a second mechanism. Strongly activated pathways inhibit neighboring pathways through central circuits, sharpening the contrast around each activity peak. This makes spatial boundaries more distinct and can improve localization and discrimination.

Do not say that receptive-field overlap always makes discrimination worse; overlapping fields can support useful population codes. The difficulty arises when spatial information is pooled too broadly to resolve the two inputs. Nor does lateral inhibition increase action-potential amplitude: it changes activity across neighboring pathways.

## w05-sr03

The all-or-none rule applies to the size of each individual action potential, **not to the number of spikes a neuron can generate over time**. A stronger effective stimulus commonly produces a larger graded receptor potential. In a depolarizing sensory pathway, that stronger drive can bring the spike-generating region to threshold more often, increasing firing frequency without enlarging each spike.

A stronger stimulus may also activate additional receptors and afferent fibers. This recruitment creates a population code: the CNS receives information both from how rapidly individual axons fire and from how many pathways are active.

Duration is conveyed by the timing and persistence of activity. Slowly adapting tonic receptors continue responding while a stimulus remains present. Rapidly adapting phasic receptors emphasize onset, offset, or changes, so their firing may decline even during a sustained stimulus. The CNS interprets these different patterns together rather than assuming that every sensory neuron must fire continuously throughout the entire stimulus.

## w05-sr04

Fast, sharp pain is carried mainly by **Aδ afferents**, which are small but myelinated. Slow, dull or burning pain is carried mainly by **C afferents**, which are small and unmyelinated.

After a painful pinprick, for example, an early, relatively well-localized sharp sensation may be followed by a later aching or burning sensation. Myelination allows faster propagation along the Aδ axon, while unmyelinated C-fiber conduction is slower. Axon diameter also affects conduction, but do not describe Aδ fibers as the largest or fastest fibers in the nervous system; other sensory and motor fibers conduct faster.

The difference in perception is not explained by speed alone. The two afferent populations also have different patterns of central connections and processing, contributing to differences in localization, persistence, and the affective quality of pain. Both can be activated by potentially damaging stimulation and both are sensory inputs rather than motor outputs.

It should not claim that fast pain uses larger action potentials or that all injuries produce exactly the same two-stage pattern.

## w05-sr05

Nociceptive information from the heart enters central pathways that can also receive somatic sensory input. When visceral and somatic afferents converge on shared central neurons, activity in those neurons is not an unambiguous label of which peripheral tissue initiated it.

The CNS commonly interprets that activity using established spatial associations with somatic input. Cardiac nociceptive activity can consequently be experienced in a chest or upper-limb territory rather than being localized precisely to the heart. Neck and jaw referral also involve patterns of central convergence and processing; they should not be reduced to a claim that every possible referral site is supplied by one identical spinal segment.

The pain has not physically travelled from the heart into the arm, and the arm need not contain the original disturbance. Nor is a change in action-potential amplitude the explanation: larger spikes would not solve the ambiguity about source.

The scenario illustrates a sensory-processing mechanism, not a rule that a particular symptom location establishes a diagnosis.

## w05-sr06

Begin in the **olfactory epithelium**, where odorant molecules dissolve in mucus and reach receptors on the cilia of olfactory receptor neurons. Unlike several other special-sense receptor cells, the olfactory receptor is itself a neuron. Binding of an odorant to a GPCR activates Golf, which stimulates adenylyl cyclase to produce cAMP.

The rise in cAMP opens cyclic-nucleotide-gated cation channels. Inward positive current depolarizes the receptor neuron; additional ionic mechanisms can amplify the response. If the depolarization is sufficient, action potentials travel along the neuron's axon.

Axons of the receptor neurons form olfactory nerve fibers and enter the olfactory bulb, where they synapse in organized circuits. Bulb output travels through the olfactory tract to primary olfactory cortical and related forebrain regions. The initial route to primary olfactory cortex does not require an obligatory first thalamic relay, although subsequent olfactory processing can involve the thalamus.

## w05-sr07

In the course's simplified account, **salty and sour transduction depend relatively directly on ions**. Sodium entry can contribute to salty responses, while hydrogen-ion-related mechanisms underlie sour responses. These mechanisms alter the taste cell's electrical state.

Sweet, bitter, and umami use a different starting strategy. Tastants bind **G-protein-coupled receptors**, activating intracellular signalling pathways that alter ion handling and depolarize the cell. The receptor therefore initiates a chemical cascade rather than acting as a voltage-gated sodium channel. Detailed channels and transmitter-release mechanisms are not identical across all taste-cell types, so avoid presenting every taste as one completely uniform molecular pathway.

Despite those differences, the functional outcome is similar: the receptor cell changes its signalling to gustatory afferents, which carry taste information toward the CNS. The taste cell is not the same thing as the primary sensory axon, and depolarization of the taste cell should not be confused with an action potential travelling the full central pathway.

## w05-sr08

Air-pressure waves enter the external ear and vibrate the tympanic membrane. The malleus, incus, and stapes transmit that motion through the middle ear, with the stapes acting at the oval window. Their arrangement improves the transfer of pressure variations from air into cochlear fluid; it does not create new energy.

Movement at the oval window produces fluid motion and a travelling disturbance along the basilar membrane. The resulting relative movement within the organ of Corti bends hair-cell stereocilia. Different parts of the basilar membrane respond most strongly to different frequencies, so mechanical frequency separation occurs before central interpretation.

When a hair bundle bends in the excitatory direction, tip-link tension opens mechanically gated channels. K⁺ enters from the potassium-rich endolymph and depolarizes the hair cell. Depolarization then opens basal voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, increasing transmitter release onto auditory afferents. Those afferents generate the action potentials that travel toward the brainstem.

Hair cells release transmitter; auditory afferents, not hair cells, carry the travelling action potentials.

## w05-sr09

Pitch and loudness correspond mainly to different physical properties of sound, so begin by separating **frequency** from **amplitude and intensity**. Pitch is associated primarily with frequency, measured in cycles per second. Loudness is a perceptual response strongly related to sound intensity, although it is not identical to the physical intensity measurement.

For pitch, the cochlea uses place coding. High-frequency sounds produce maximal movement nearer the stiff base of the basilar membrane, whereas lower-frequency sounds peak nearer its more flexible apex. Hair cells at those locations activate corresponding afferent pathways, preserving information about which cochlear region responded. Temporal patterns also contribute to frequency coding, particularly at lower frequencies, but the course's central organizing principle is place.

For loudness, stronger sound-related movement produces stronger receptor responses, often increasing firing rates in auditory afferents and recruiting additional fibers. The CNS reads the resulting pattern across the neural population. Individual action potentials do not become proportionally taller as sounds get louder.

## w05-sr10

Start with transmitter release from cochlear hair cells onto primary auditory afferents. Their axons travel in the cochlear division of cranial nerve VIII and first enter the **cochlear nuclei in the brainstem**. Ascending auditory pathways then distribute information through bilateral brainstem circuits, including the superior olivary complex, and continue through midbrain relays such as the inferior colliculus, the medial geniculate region of the thalamus, and auditory cortex.

The bilateral organization means that many central neurons can receive information related to both ears. Comparing the arrival times and levels of a sound at the two ears helps the nervous system estimate its direction.

Bilateral projections also mean that damage to a single central auditory pathway does not generally produce the same simple pattern as loss of input from one auditory nerve. However, this should not be exaggerated into a claim that central damage cannot impair hearing or localization. Effects depend on the location and extent of the disturbance.

## w05-sr11

Both somatic and special senses use the same broad logic: a receptor detects an appropriate stimulus, transduces it into an electrical response, and supplies afferent information for CNS processing. They differ principally in **where the receptors are concentrated and how the surrounding structures specialize the input**.

Somatic receptors are distributed through the skin, muscles, tendons, and joints. They provide information about touch, temperature, pain, and body position. Somatic sensation from much of the body enters through spinal pathways, while sensation from the face also uses cranial pathways; it is therefore inaccurate to equate all somatic input with spinal nerves alone.

Special senses use concentrated, specialized receptor arrangements such as the retina, olfactory epithelium, taste buds, and inner-ear structures. Their associated organs may focus light, transmit sound, or bring chemicals into contact with receptor cells. Their afferent pathways primarily use cranial nerves.

Some receptors, such as olfactory receptor neurons, are neurons themselves, while others, such as auditory hair cells, signal to separate afferents.
