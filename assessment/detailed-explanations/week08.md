## w08-mc01

**Arterioles are the major adjustable resistance vessels**, so B is correct. Their relatively small lumen and substantial smooth-muscle control allow changes in radius to produce large changes in resistance. By constricting or dilating, they regulate how much blood enters downstream tissue beds and contribute importantly to total peripheral resistance.

Elastic arteries in A mainly conduct blood under pressure and store elastic energy during ventricular ejection. Capillaries in C provide thin exchange surfaces. Although an individual capillary is very narrow, the enormous number of capillaries arranged in parallel means that the capillary network should not be identified as the main adjustable resistance system simply from one vessel's diameter.

Large veins in D serve as low-pressure return pathways and volume reservoirs. Their tone can affect venous return, but that is not the principal resistance-vessel role tested here. ## w08-mc02

Use the flow relationship **Q = ΔP/R**, where ΔP is the pressure difference between the upstream and downstream ends of the vascular bed. If resistance stays fixed, an increase in that driving difference produces an increase in flow, making A correct.

For example, with a resistance of 10 mmHg·min/L, a gradient of 50 mmHg produces 5 L/min, while a gradient of 70 mmHg produces 7 L/min. These are illustrative values showing the proportional relationship, not a claim about a particular organ. The units also show that pressure divided by resistance gives a volume flow rate.

The relevant quantity is the **difference**, not an isolated pressure reading. Raising upstream and downstream pressure by the same amount would leave ΔP unchanged. Flow would reverse only if the sign of the pressure difference reversed, not simply because its magnitude increased. The wrong choices either reverse the relationship, assume no driving effect, or introduce a reversal that the question does not specify.

## w08-mc03

**A is correct because resistance rises steeply as vessel radius decreases.** In the idealized Poiseuille relationship, resistance is proportional to viscosity and vessel length but inversely proportional to the fourth power of radius. With other factors fixed, a modest change in radius can therefore have a much larger relative effect on resistance.

For example, reducing radius to 80% of its original value changes resistance by a factor of 1/(0.8⁴), approximately 2.44. At the same pressure gradient, flow would fall to about 41% of its previous value. This illustrates why arteriolar smooth muscle can regulate flow so effectively.

Option B gives the opposite direction, while C ignores the strong radius dependence. Viscosity remains part of the relationship, so D is also wrong. Real vessels are compliant and blood is not a perfect ideal fluid, so the equation is a useful model rather than an exact universal description. Nevertheless, the robust principle is **smaller lumen → greater resistance → less flow at a fixed pressure gradient**.

## w08-mc04

**B is correct because the capillary network has the greatest total cross-sectional area.** Average blood velocity is related to volume flow and area by v = Q/A. For the same total flow through successive portions of a circuit, spreading that flow across a much larger combined area reduces the average speed.

The crucial word is **total**. One capillary is much narrower than the aorta, but blood is distributed among an enormous number of capillaries in parallel. Their combined cross-sectional area is much larger than that of the single large upstream vessel. Slow transit also supports the exchange of gases, nutrients, and wastes between blood and tissues.

The aorta, vena cava, and pulmonary trunk have much smaller combined areas than the relevant capillary networks, so average velocity is higher in those large vessels. Do not confuse low velocity with low total flow: in a steady circuit, the same overall volume can pass each level per minute even though the speed within individual vessels differs greatly.

## w08-mc05

**Diffusion down concentration gradients** is the main exchange mechanism for many small solutes, making A correct. Oxygen can diffuse from blood toward tissues where its concentration or partial pressure is lower, while carbon dioxide commonly moves in the opposite direction. Lipid-soluble substances can cross endothelial cell membranes, and many small water-soluble substances use available aqueous pathways through the capillary wall.

Diffusion is different from **bulk flow**, in which fluid and accompanying dissolved substances move because of pressure-related forces. Bulk flow is particularly important for the balance of water between plasma and interstitial fluid, but it does not replace diffusion as the main explanation for much small-solute exchange.

The precise route depends on the substance and the capillary's permeability, so not every molecule crosses every capillary equally. Skeletal muscle contraction may affect circulation, but it is not the direct transendothelial exchange mechanism. Action potentials and coagulation likewise do not explain routine movement of these solutes. Identify the **driving gradient and transport mechanism**, not merely a process that occurs nearby.

## w08-mc06

**Lymphatic vessels return excess interstitial fluid and escaped proteins to the circulation**, making A correct. Capillary filtration moves some fluid out of the vascular compartment. Not all of that fluid is reclaimed directly through blood-capillary reabsorption, and proteins that reach the interstitial space also require a return route.

Initial lymphatics collect this material and carry it through lymphatic vessels toward its eventual return to venous blood. This helps maintain tissue-fluid balance and prevents a continuing net transfer of fluid and protein out of the circulation. When lymphatic drainage is inadequate relative to the filtered load, interstitial fluid can accumulate as edema.

Pacemaker action potentials, ACh release at neuromuscular junctions, and sound generation are unrelated to this fluid-return role. Lymphatics also have other functions, including immune transport, but the question concerns their cardiovascular contribution. The key is to view blood capillaries, interstitial fluid, and lymphatic vessels as **connected parts of a fluid-balance system**, rather than assuming that all filtered fluid automatically re-enters the same capillary.

## w08-mc07

**A is correct because elastic arteries act as a pressure reservoir.** During ventricular ejection, part of the stroke volume stretches their walls. Some of the heart's mechanical energy is temporarily stored as elastic energy rather than being transmitted only as a brief surge of flow.

During diastole, when the ventricle is no longer ejecting into the aorta, the stretched arterial walls recoil. That recoil helps maintain arterial pressure and drive blood onward through the peripheral circulation. Flow therefore continues between beats rather than stopping every time ventricular ejection ends.

This does not eliminate systolic pressure, as B suggests. Instead, arterial compliance moderates the pressure change produced by a given ejected volume. AV valves respond to the pressure difference between atria and ventricles, so recoil does not directly command their opening. Red-cell production is a separate process. Distinguish **the ventricle as the source of pumping energy** from **the arteries as temporary stores that smooth its delivery over the cardiac cycle**.

## w08-mc08

$$PP = 120 - 75 = 45\;\text{mmHg}.$$

At an ordinary resting heart rate, the course approximation is:

$$MAP \approx DBP + \frac{PP}{3} = 75 + \frac{45}{3} = 90\;\text{mmHg}.$$

## w08-mc09

**A identifies the relationship between mean arterial pressure, cardiac output, and total peripheral resistance.** Starting from Q = ΔP/R for the systemic circulation gives MAP − right atrial pressure = CO × TPR. Because right atrial pressure is usually small relative to MAP, the course commonly simplifies this to MAP ≈ CO × TPR.

This equation explains why pressure depends on both the amount of blood pumped per minute and the resistance opposing its flow. Greater CO at a fixed TPR tends to raise MAP; greater TPR at a fixed CO also tends to raise MAP. In the intact circulation, the variables can change together, so the assumptions matter.

EDV − ESV in B defines stroke volume, not pressure. HR/SV in C is not the cardiac-output relationship, which uses multiplication. Subtracting plasma and red cells in D does not yield a pressure. Connecting **CO = HR × SV** with the MAP relationship provides a framework for following how cardiac, vascular, and volume changes affect arterial pressure.

## w08-mc10

An acute loss of circulating volume reduces filling of the venous system and tends to reduce the pressure gradient driving blood back to the heart. **Venous return falls, ventricular filling and EDV fall, and stroke volume tends to decrease through the Frank–Starling relationship.** A is therefore correct.

At an initially unchanged heart rate, the fall in SV reduces CO because CO = HR × SV. If resistance is initially unchanged as well, the decrease in CO lowers MAP. This is the initiating hemodynamic sequence before compensatory responses are fully considered.

The baroreflex can subsequently raise heart rate, contractility, and vascular tone, partly opposing the pressure fall. That compensation does not make the initial effect of volume loss disappear as a physiological mechanism. Phototransduction and nicotinic receptors are unrelated to the direct volume-to-pressure pathway. ## w08-mc11

**Myogenic autoregulation is an intrinsic response of vascular smooth muscle to stretch**, making A correct. When perfusion pressure rises, an arteriole tends to be stretched. The smooth muscle responds by contracting, reducing radius and increasing resistance. This opposes the increase in flow that the higher pressure would otherwise produce.

When pressure and stretch fall, reduced myogenic contraction can allow dilation, lowering resistance and helping maintain flow. The response works over a limited physiological range; it does not guarantee perfectly constant flow at every possible pressure.

This mechanism differs from a centrally organized autonomic reflex. It can occur locally in the vessel without waiting for a new command from the medulla. Motor-neuron ACh at a skeletal neuromuscular junction in B is not the required trigger. Light detection and fibrin production are unrelated. The apparent paradox is resolved by distinguishing the initial **passive stretching effect of pressure** from the subsequent **active contraction that counteracts the flow change**.

## w08-mc14

As arterial pressure changes, wall stretch changes, altering their afferent firing and informing brainstem cardiovascular circuits about the pressure state.

Carotid sinus signals travel mainly through glossopharyngeal pathways, and aortic arch signals through vagal pathways, to medullary processing centers. The resulting reflex can adjust heart rate, contractility, and vascular tone rapidly. The receptors are therefore sensors in a feedback loop, not structures that generate the corrective motor commands themselves.

## w08-mc15

**A is correct: a fall in arterial pressure reduces wall stretch and usually decreases arterial baroreceptor firing.** The sensory endings report the changed mechanical condition through a lower rate of afferent action potentials. Individual spikes do not become smaller to encode the pressure fall; the firing pattern changes.

The medullary cardiovascular circuits respond to this reduced afferent input by increasing sympathetic activity and reducing parasympathetic influence on the heart. Those outputs help raise heart rate, strengthen contraction, and increase vascular tone, opposing the initial pressure decrease.

A common mistake is to jump directly to the compensatory increase in sympathetic activity and assume that the sensory receptor must also increase its firing. The two changes are opposite: **less baroreceptor input leads to more sympathetic corrective output**. Increased firing in B would be more consistent with greater arterial stretch. Fixed firing in C would not report the disturbance, and sensory afferent activity does not become motor output in D; the CNS processes input before producing a separate output.

## w08-mc16

## w08-mc18

## w08-mc20

**Hematopoiesis means the production and differentiation of blood cells**, making A correct. In adults, this process occurs principally in bone marrow, where hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells give rise to the blood-cell lineages. It continually replaces cells that are lost or reach the end of their lifespan.

## w08-sr01

Compare each vessel type by linking **wall structure, pressure environment, and function**. Large arteries carry blood away from the heart under relatively high pressure. Their strong walls withstand that pressure, and elastic arteries stretch during ejection and recoil during diastole, smoothing the delivery of blood between beats.

Arterioles have small lumens and actively controlled smooth muscle. A complete answer does more than arrange names in sequence. ## w08-sr02

The relationship **Q = ΔP/R** states that flow equals the pressure difference driving blood through a vascular segment divided by the resistance opposing that flow. Increasing the gradient at a fixed resistance increases flow; increasing resistance at a fixed gradient decreases flow. Pressure at a single location is not enough to determine the driving force.

For example, upstream and downstream pressures of 100 and 20 mmHg produce an 80-mmHg gradient. Raising both to 110 and 30 mmHg leaves the gradient unchanged. This shows why a higher upstream reading alone does not necessarily imply more flow when other pressures also change.

Arteriolar radius is especially influential because the idealized Poiseuille model gives R proportional to 1/r⁴ when viscosity and length are held fixed. Halving radius would increase modeled resistance sixteenfold and reduce flow to one-sixteenth at the same gradient. Dilation has the opposite effect. The numerical example illustrates the model's strong dependence, not an exact prediction for every real vascular network.

Distinguish a local vascular bed from the entire circulation. Local constriction can reduce that bed's flow, while widespread arteriolar constriction can raise total peripheral resistance and support MAP. A good explanation explicitly states the **variables being held constant** rather than assuming that pressure, output, and resistance cannot interact.

## w08-sr03

Use the continuity relationship **v = Q/A**: average velocity equals volume flow divided by the total cross-sectional area carrying that flow. Although a single capillary has a tiny diameter, an enormous number of capillaries carry blood in parallel. Their combined area is much larger than the area of a single large artery.

In a steady circuit, comparable total volumes pass successive levels per minute. When that flow is distributed across the capillary network's large combined area, the average speed becomes low. As vessels merge on the venous side and combined area decreases, average velocity can rise again. No contradiction exists between slow capillary velocity and maintenance of overall circulatory flow.

The slow transit supports exchange by giving blood time to interact with a large, thin-walled surface. Oxygen and other solutes still move according to their gradients and permeability, so slow velocity is helpful but is not itself the mechanism of diffusion.

The main mistake is to look only at one capillary and conclude that its narrow diameter must make blood move faster than in the aorta. That ignores how the total flow is divided among parallel vessels. A full explanation connects **many parallel capillaries → large total area → low average velocity → conditions favorable for exchange**.

## w08-sr04

Bulk flow is the movement of fluid across the capillary wall because of the balance of **hydrostatic and colloid osmotic forces**. Capillary hydrostatic pressure tends to push fluid outward. Plasma proteins, which are relatively retained within the vascular compartment, create an oncotic influence that favors movement toward plasma. Interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic pressures also contribute to the net balance.

In the simplified Starling-force account used for introductory reasoning, net outward movement is filtration and net inward movement is absorption. Permeability and effective exchange surface also matter, so pressure values alone are not a complete description of every capillary bed. This fluid movement should be distinguished from diffusion of solutes down concentration gradients.

Lymphatics provide the essential return route for excess filtered fluid and escaped proteins. They collect interstitial fluid and eventually return it to venous blood. Do not assume that every capillary continuously filters at one end and reabsorbs every drop at the other: patterns vary, and lymphatic return is necessary for normal fluid balance.

Edema can develop when filtration exceeds the combined capacity for absorption and lymphatic removal. Increased capillary pressure, reduced effective plasma oncotic pressure, increased permeability, or impaired lymphatic drainage can shift that balance. A complete answer explains **the competing forces, the direction of net fluid movement, and the route by which excess fluid returns**.

## w08-sr05

**Mean arterial pressure is the time-averaged arterial pressure over a cardiac cycle.** It contributes to the driving pressure for systemic blood flow, although the actual systemic gradient is MAP minus right atrial pressure. At ordinary resting heart rates, it can be estimated as diastolic pressure plus one-third of pulse pressure, where pulse pressure is systolic minus diastolic pressure.

The one-third estimate reflects the longer time normally spent in diastole; it is not a simple arithmetic average of the systolic and diastolic readings. For 120/75 mmHg, pulse pressure is 45 mmHg and estimated MAP is 75 + 15 = 90 mmHg. A changed heart rate or waveform can affect the approximation.

The systemic flow relationship gives MAP − right atrial pressure = CO × TPR, commonly simplified to **MAP ≈ CO × TPR** when right atrial pressure is small. Cardiac output is HR × SV, and stroke volume is EDV − ESV. Heart rate, filling, contractility, and arteriolar tone can therefore influence MAP through different links.

Blood volume affects venous return and filling; arteriolar radius strongly affects resistance. A strong answer links the equations to those mechanisms rather than presenting MAP as determined by heart rate alone or treating pressure and flow as the same quantity.

## w08-sr06

Both mechanisms adjust arteriolar tone locally, but they begin with different signals. **Myogenic autoregulation** responds to stretch of the vessel wall. A rise in perfusion pressure stretches smooth muscle, which contracts and raises resistance, opposing the initial tendency for flow to increase. Reduced stretch can lead to relaxation and lower resistance. This helps stabilize flow over an appropriate pressure range.

**Metabolic and paracrine control** responds to the tissue environment. Increased metabolic activity can lower local oxygen availability and change concentrations of metabolites and locally released signalling molecules. In many systemic tissues, these influences promote dilation, lowering resistance and increasing delivery to match demand. This is the logic of active hyperemia.

Reactive hyperemia illustrates the same chemical principle after a period of reduced flow: accumulated vasodilatory influences can produce temporarily elevated flow when the restriction is removed. The purpose is not identical to myogenic stabilization; one response helps resist a pressure-driven change, while the other adjusts supply to local conditions.

Neither mechanism requires every adjustment to await a new central reflex command, although both interact with autonomic influences. A complete comparison identifies **stimulus, vessel response, effect on resistance, and functional goal**. Avoid calling every local dilation a myogenic response or assuming that sympathetic tone overrides all tissue-level regulation in the same way everywhere.

## w08-sr07

Begin with the disturbance sensed by the receptors: a fall in MAP reduces stretch of the **carotid sinus and aortic arch**. Their baroreceptor endings consequently fire less frequently. Carotid sinus afferents travel mainly through glossopharyngeal pathways, and aortic afferents through vagal pathways, to medullary cardiovascular processing centers.

The medulla responds with **increased sympathetic output and reduced cardiac parasympathetic output**. At the SA node, greater β1 influence and less M2-mediated inhibition raise heart rate. In ventricular myocardium, sympathetic signalling increases calcium-dependent contractility. These changes support cardiac output through HR and SV.

Sympathetic activity also constricts many systemic arterioles through α1 receptors, raising total peripheral resistance. Venoconstriction reduces venous capacitance and supports return to the heart, helping maintain EDV and stroke volume. Combining increased CO and TPR through MAP ≈ CO × TPR explains how the reflex opposes the initial pressure fall.

Label the sensor, afferent limb, integrating center, efferent autonomic pathways, and target organs separately in a flow chart. The critical directional contrast is **baroreceptor firing decreases while sympathetic output increases**. As pressure and stretch recover, the initiating sensory disturbance is reduced, completing negative feedback. The reflex can redistribute blood and change pump activity quickly, but it does not itself replace fluid or blood lost from the body.

## w08-sr08

In the teaching scenario, severe vomiting and diarrhea remove water and solutes from the body. If the loss is not replaced, circulating plasma volume can fall. The venous system then supplies less effective filling pressure and **venous return decreases**, reducing ventricular EDV.

Within the normal Frank–Starling range, reduced filling and stretch lead to a smaller stroke volume. With HR initially unchanged, CO = HR × SV predicts a fall in cardiac output. With TPR initially unchanged, MAP ≈ CO × TPR then predicts a fall in mean arterial pressure. The chain is therefore **fluid loss → lower circulating volume → lower venous return and EDV → lower SV → lower CO → lower MAP**.

The baroreflex attempts to oppose this sequence by increasing heart rate, contractility, and vascular tone. Thus an increased heart rate does not prove that output or pressure has been fully restored; a sufficiently large fall in stroke volume can outweigh the rate increase. Continued standing and venous pooling can further challenge return in a volume-depleted person.

If cerebral perfusion becomes inadequate, fainting can occur. This explanation shows how the supplied scenario connects volume balance to brain function; it is not a claim that all fainting has the same cause. Full credit requires the intermediate links and equations, rather than simply saying that dehydration lowers blood pressure.

## w08-sr09

Blood consists of **plasma and formed elements**. Plasma is mostly water containing proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste products. The formed elements are erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets. Red cells specialize in gas transport, white cells participate in defense and immune functions, and platelets contribute to hemostasis.

When anticoagulated blood is centrifuged, the denser red cells form a lower layer. Plasma lies above, and a thin buffy coat containing white cells and platelets sits between them. This arrangement distinguishes components physically rather than relying only on a list of names.

**Hematocrit is the fraction or percentage of total blood volume occupied by red cells.** If red cells occupy 45 mL in a 100-mL blood sample, the hematocrit is 45%. It is not the percentage of blood that is hemoglobin, nor the combined volume of all formed elements. Hemoglobin concentration and hematocrit are related measurements but are not identical.

Hematocrit can change because red-cell volume changes or because plasma volume changes. A rise during plasma-water loss does not necessarily mean that new red cells were rapidly produced. That distinction makes hematocrit a useful example of why a proportion must be interpreted in relation to both its numerator and its denominator.

## w08-sr10

**Hematopoiesis** is the production of blood cells from stem and progenitor populations, occurring principally in adult bone marrow. Different growth factors and cytokines support the development of different lineages. Erythropoiesis is the red-cell branch of that broader process.

When oxygen availability to renal sensing cells decreases, the kidneys increase production of **erythropoietin (EPO)**. EPO travels through the bloodstream to bone marrow and promotes survival and development of erythroid precursors. As more red cells enter circulation, the capacity to carry oxygen can increase, provided the marrow has the required resources and is functioning appropriately.

Improved oxygen delivery reduces the initiating signal for EPO, creating a negative-feedback relationship. The kidneys are responding to their oxygen environment rather than simply measuring a universal red-cell count. Changes in oxygenation, hemoglobin availability, or perfusion can therefore affect the signal.

This response operates on a different time scale from a baroreflex. EPO does not immediately convert plasma into red cells or restore a hemorrhage within seconds; cell development takes time. Nor does EPO principally initiate platelet clot formation. A complete explanation identifies **the sensed variable, endocrine source, circulating signal, marrow target, slower production response, and feedback outcome**.

## w08-sr11

Primary hemostasis begins when vascular damage exposes adhesive structures and alters the local endothelial environment. **Platelets adhere**, including through interactions with exposed matrix and von Willebrand factor, and become activated. They change shape, release mediators, and alter surface binding properties, allowing additional platelets to aggregate into a plug.

This is locally amplified: activated platelets help recruit and activate more platelets. Fibrinogen can help bridge activated platelet receptors during aggregation, but that bridging role should not be confused with the later conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin. The initial cellular plug and the stabilizing protein network are related but distinct.

The coagulation system generates **thrombin**, which converts soluble fibrinogen into fibrin strands. Fibrin reinforces the platelet plug, and further stabilization helps produce a durable clot. Platelet surfaces also support coagulation reactions, so the two processes cooperate rather than forming completely independent pathways.

The response must remain localized. Intact endothelium, anticoagulant mechanisms, blood flow, and later fibrinolysis help limit clotting and permit restoration of the vessel. A strong answer therefore explains **adhesion → activation → aggregation → fibrin reinforcement**, while recognizing positive feedback during plug growth and the mechanisms that prevent clotting from spreading indefinitely throughout normal vessels.

## w08-sr12

For **ABO red-cell compatibility**, distinguish donor-cell antigens from recipient plasma antibodies. Type A cells carry A antigen, type B cells carry B, AB cells carry both, and group O cells carry neither A nor B. The corresponding normal antibody pattern can cause recipient plasma to react with incompatible donor red cells, producing aggregation and hemolysis. This framework concerns red-cell transfusion; plasma compatibility follows a different direction of antibody exposure, and actual matching involves more than ABO alone.

The **Rh(D) system** is separate. An Rh-negative person lacks D antigen and does not normally have pre-existing anti-D in the same way that ABO antibodies are commonly present. Exposure to D-positive red cells can induce sensitization and an acquired anti-D response.

During pregnancy, fetal D-positive red cells can enter an Rh-negative mother's circulation. If maternal sensitization occurs, anti-D IgG can cross the placenta and damage D-positive fetal red cells in an affected pregnancy. Prior exposure can arise from an earlier pregnancy or another blood-cell exposure; the risk should not be simplified to a rule that only a second child can be affected.

Prophylactic anti-D in an unsensitized mother suppresses the development of her own anti-D response. It supplies passive antibody rather than encouraging immune memory, and it does not erase an established response. The unifying principle is **which antigen is present, which antibody can recognize it, and which compartment the antibody can reach**.
