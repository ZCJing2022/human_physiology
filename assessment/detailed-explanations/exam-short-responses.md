## e01-q25

The observation is a decline in firing **despite continued pressure**, so the relevant property is sensory adaptation. The receptor and its associated transduction machinery become less responsive to an unchanging stimulus over time. The afferent can therefore fire rapidly at onset and then slow down even though the external pressure has not disappeared. A particularly strong decline is characteristic of phasic behavior, although the exact response depends on the receptor type.

This does not contradict the all-or-none nature of action potentials. Each spike remains a stereotyped electrical event; what changes is the number of spikes per second. The graded drive produced by the receptor and the spike-generating process determine that firing rate. Do not describe the later spikes as becoming proportionally smaller.

The CNS does not have to infer stimulus duration from this one rapidly adapting channel alone. Slowly adapting tonic receptors can continue responding throughout the maintained pressure, while phasic receptors emphasize transitions such as onset and, in some receptor types, offset. The population's combined timing patterns indicate both that contact began and that it persists.

A falling firing rate during sustained pressure indicates adaptation, not necessarily a weaker stimulus.

## e01-q26

In darkness, cGMP keeps outer-segment cation channels open. The resulting inward current helps maintain the rod at a relatively depolarized membrane potential, which supports ongoing calcium entry at its synaptic terminal and tonic glutamate release.

Light changes this arrangement through a receptor cascade. Photon absorption activates rhodopsin, which activates the G protein transducin. Transducin activates phosphodiesterase, and that enzyme breaks down cGMP. With less cGMP available, cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels close and the inward positive current decreases.

Potassium efflux continues, so the balance shifts toward a more negative membrane voltage: **the rod hyperpolarizes**. Hyperpolarization reduces opening of synaptic voltage-gated calcium channels. Less terminal calcium entry means less vesicular glutamate release. The reduced transmitter output is the rod's signal that illumination has increased.

The explanation must distinguish outer-segment transduction channels from the synaptic calcium channels involved in release. It should also distinguish a graded receptor response from the ganglion-cell action potentials that later travel in the optic nerve. Reduced glutamate does not mean that every downstream retinal neuron is inhibited; ON and OFF circuits interpret the change differently.

## e01-q27

The problem says that motor-neuron action potentials are normal, so the presynaptic electrical signal still arrives. Assuming the toxin acts specifically at the postsynaptic nicotinic receptor, presynaptic calcium entry and ACh release can also remain intact. The defect is not initially a failure of the motor neuron to fire.

ACh normally opens nicotinic cation channels at the motor end plate, producing a graded end-plate potential. Receptor blockade reduces this current. If the end-plate potential no longer reaches the threshold needed to activate nearby voltage-gated sodium channels, the muscle action potential fails. With partial blockade, some responses may remain; complete failure is the limiting case when the safety margin is lost sufficiently.

Without a muscle action potential, depolarization does not propagate normally down the T-tubules. The voltage sensor therefore does not trigger the usual SR calcium release. Cytosolic calcium fails to rise adequately, troponin is not activated sufficiently, and tropomyosin continues to restrict actin–myosin interaction. Force decreases or disappears in the affected fibers.

This is distinct from a toxin acting directly on SR release: that later defect could preserve the muscle action potential while preventing contraction.

## e01-q28

The question contrasts two independent control dimensions: **how frequently active fibers are stimulated** and **how many fibers are active**. A skeletal muscle action potential is much shorter than the calcium-dependent twitch it initiates. A second action potential can therefore occur while calcium and force from the first response have not fully returned to baseline.

Repeated calcium release maintains greater activation of troponin and permits more sustained cross-bridge activity. Mechanical responses summate, producing greater force than isolated twitches. At sufficiently high frequency, relaxation between responses becomes very small and fused tetanus can develop. The action potentials themselves do not merge into progressively taller spikes; the longer-lasting mechanical response is what sums.

Each motor unit comprises a motor neuron and its associated muscle fibers. Activating additional units adds more contracting fibers to the whole muscle. Smaller, lower-threshold units are generally recruited first, with larger units joining as neural drive and force requirements increase. This gives graded control instead of an all-or-none activation of the whole muscle.

Both strategies can operate together. An already active unit can fire more frequently while additional units are recruited.

## e02-q27

Normal cardiac excitation begins in the **SA node**, whose spontaneous rhythm usually reaches threshold faster than other potential pacemakers. Excitation spreads through the atria, producing atrial depolarization and initiating atrial contraction. It then reaches the **AV node**, where conduction slows before entering the ventricular conduction system.

After the delay, the signal travels through the **bundle of His**, the **right and left bundle branches**, and **Purkinje fibers** into the ventricular myocardium. The rapid distal distribution helps different ventricular regions activate in an organized pattern so that contraction can generate useful ejection rather than poorly coordinated local force.

It leaves time for the atria to make their final contribution to ventricular filling before ventricular contraction raises pressure and closes the AV valves. Most filling at rest is passive, but coordinating the atrial contribution with the next ventricular systole improves the sequence of pump activity.

The AV node does not actively open a valve, and its delay is not the same as the entire ventricular filling period. Electrical activation changes muscle activity; the resulting pressure differences determine valve position and blood flow.

## e02-q28

The **QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization**, not blood ejection itself. Depolarization initiates excitation–contraction coupling, raising cytosolic calcium and activating the contractile machinery. After this electrical event, ventricular tension and pressure begin to rise.

Initially, ventricular pressure becomes greater than atrial pressure. That pressure reversal closes the AV valves and is associated with the first heart sound, S1. The semilunar valves remain closed because the pressure in the aorta or pulmonary artery is still greater than ventricular pressure. The ventricle is therefore in **isovolumetric contraction**: pressure rises while the blood volume stays at EDV because both inlet and outlet valves are closed.

As contraction continues, ventricular pressure eventually exceeds the corresponding arterial pressure. The semilunar valve opens, establishing a forward route for blood. **Ejection begins at this pressure crossing**, and ventricular volume starts to fall. On the left-heart Wiggers diagram, compare left ventricular pressure with aortic pressure to locate this transition.

Do not collapse all of these into one instantaneous event at the start of the QRS. The electrical waveform provides a time reference, but valves respond to pressures, not directly to ECG labels.

## e03-q27

Solve the problem in two stages because cardiac output requires stroke volume, which is not given directly. **EDV of 125 mL** is the volume in the ventricle after filling, and **ESV of 55 mL** is what remains after ejection. The amount that left during one beat is the difference:

$$SV = EDV - ESV = 125\;\text{mL} - 55\;\text{mL} = 70\;\text{mL/beat}.$$

Now use the heart rate of **80 beats/min** to convert a per-beat volume into a per-minute flow:

$$CO = HR\times SV = 80\;\frac{\text{beats}}{\text{min}}\times70\;\frac{\text{mL}}{\text{beat}} = 5600\;\text{mL/min} = 5.6\;\text{L/min}.$$

The beat units cancel, leaving the correct flow units. Convert milliliters to liters by dividing by 1000, not by 100.

Common errors are to use EDV as the amount ejected, use ESV as stroke volume, add EDV and ESV, or add HR to a volume. The ventricle is not emptied completely, and the two chamber volumes describe its contents at different times. The final answer is **SV = 70 mL/beat and CO = 5.6 L/min**, with both equations shown so the reasoning is reproducible.

## e03-q28

The initiating change is **greater venous return**, meaning more blood arrives for ventricular filling. Under comparable filling time and otherwise normal conditions, this increases EDV and stretches the myocardium more before the next contraction. The Frank–Starling response is the intrinsic ability of cardiac muscle to generate a stronger contraction and eject a larger stroke volume when it begins at a greater filling length within its normal operating range.

With heart rate initially unchanged, CO = HR × SV predicts an increase in cardiac output. The heart thereby tends to pump forward the additional blood that has returned to it, helping match inflow and outflow.

Do not assume that ESV must remain exactly constant or fall by a specified amount; the problem gives no numerical loading conditions from which to calculate that change. The requested prediction is directional and depends on the usual physiological range, not unlimited stretching. Very abnormal filling or changes in afterload could modify the response.

The mechanism also does not require sympathetic activation as its first cause. Sympathetic positive inotropy can increase force at the same preload by changing calcium handling, but that is a different explanation.
