## w06-mc01

After the lens it travels through the vitreous humor before reaching the retina. The option omits the vitreous but preserves the correct order of the listed structures.

## w06-mc02

Curved corneal and lens surfaces use this effect to bend incoming rays so that an image can be focused on the retina.

## w06-mc03

## w06-mc04

## w06-mc05

## w06-mc07

## w06-mc10

At the skeletal neuromuscular junction, acetylcholine binds **nicotinic ACh receptors**, which are ligand-gated, nonselective cation channels. **B is correct.** At the muscle fiber's resting membrane potential, opening these channels produces a net inward positive current, mainly because sodium entry exceeds the opposing potassium movement. This depolarization is the end-plate potential.

The end-plate potential is graded. If it brings adjacent excitable membrane to threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels generate an all-or-none muscle action potential. The nicotinic receptor therefore initiates the local depolarization but is not itself the voltage-gated channel responsible for propagating the muscle action potential.

Muscarinic M2 receptors in A are important in parasympathetic cardiac signalling, not the skeletal motor end plate. Alpha1 receptors in C respond to adrenergic signalling, and rhodopsin in D initiates phototransduction.

## w06-mc11

## w06-mc12

A **sarcomere runs from one Z disc to the next**, so A is correct. It is the repeating contractile unit within a myofibril. Thin actin filaments attach to the Z discs and extend toward the center, while thick myosin filaments occupy the central region around the M line.

During shortening, thin filaments slide farther between thick filaments and the Z discs move closer together. The sarcomere becomes shorter even though the filaments themselves remain essentially the same length.

The other options confuse levels of organization. Motor neurons are nerve cells, the sarcoplasmic reticulum and sarcolemma are membrane systems, and tendons attach whole muscles to other structures. None of those pairs defines the boundaries of one repeating myofibrillar unit.

## w06-mc13

The **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)** is a specialized intracellular membrane network that stores and releases calcium, making A correct. At rest, most calcium needed for activation is stored outside the cytosol. Excitation–contraction coupling opens SR release channels, allowing cytosolic Ca²⁺ to rise quickly and activate the contractile machinery.

Relaxation requires the reverse movement. SERCA pumps use ATP to return calcium to the SR, lowering its cytosolic concentration so that calcium dissociates from troponin and cross-bridge activation declines.

Acetylcholine is synthesized and released by the motor-neuron terminal, not primarily by the muscle SR. Oxygen transport belongs mainly to blood and oxygen-binding proteins, while sound detection belongs to sensory receptors. Do not confuse the SR with T-tubules: **the SR supplies stored calcium; T-tubules carry membrane depolarization into the fiber**.

## w06-mc14

A signal confined to a muscle fiber's surface would not efficiently activate its internal myofibrils. **T-tubules carry the muscle action potential deep into the fiber**, making A correct. They are inward extensions of the sarcolemma, with their lumen continuous with extracellular space.

At triads, a T-tubule lies next to terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Depolarization activates voltage sensors in the T-tubule membrane, which are coupled to calcium-release channels in the nearby SR. This arrangement allows a surface electrical event to trigger coordinated calcium release throughout the cell.

T-tubules do not store hemoglobin, function only as ATP-producing structures, or form myelin. They also should not be described as the main calcium-storage compartment: that is the SR.

## w06-mc15

Myosin-binding sites on actin become more accessible, allowing cross-bridges to form and cycle.

Calcium therefore acts as a **regulatory signal**: it permits the motor proteins to interact. It does not replace ATP as the energy source, and it does not need to bind directly to myosin ATPase to initiate the normal skeletal-muscle regulatory sequence.

Titin in C contributes elastic and structural support, including stabilization of thick filaments. Nebulin in D helps organize thin filaments. Neither is the main calcium-sensitive switch identified in this question.

## w06-mc16

The question specifically asks about **ATP binding**, not ATP hydrolysis. When a new ATP molecule binds to myosin, myosin's affinity for actin decreases and the cross-bridge detaches. **A is correct.** Hydrolysis of that ATP subsequently places the myosin head in an energized, re-cocked state that can participate in another cycle.

If actin sites remain available because calcium is still bound to troponin, the head can reattach. Release of inorganic phosphate is associated with the force-generating transition, and ADP is released as the cycle proceeds.

Calcium, not ATP binding to troponin, exposes the binding sites. Nicotinic receptors respond to ACh, and a motor-neuron action potential is a separate electrical process. In the absence of ATP, attached cross-bridges cannot detach normally, contributing to rigor.

## w06-mc18

## w06-mc19

Relaxation requires cytosolic calcium to decline so that it dissociates from troponin. **SERCA pumps move Ca²⁺ from the cytosol back into the SR using ATP**, making B correct. ## w06-mc20

A single action potential in a resting skeletal muscle fiber normally initiates one brief contraction–relaxation response called a **twitch**, so A is correct. The electrical event first triggers SR calcium release. Calcium activates troponin, cross-bridges generate force, and subsequent calcium reuptake allows the force to decline.

The mechanical response lasts longer than the membrane action potential because calcium handling and cross-bridge cycling continue after the brief electrical event has ended.

Tetanus in C requires sufficiently frequent repeated stimulation, not one isolated action potential. A cardiac cycle and an ECG P wave describe events in the heart rather than a skeletal fiber's twitch. ## w06-mc21

**Temporal summation occurs when another stimulus arrives before the muscle has fully relaxed**, making A correct. The second muscle action potential triggers additional calcium release while some calcium and force from the first response remain.

What sums is the **mechanical response and its calcium-dependent activation**, not the amplitudes of overlapping action potentials. At sufficiently high stimulation rates, relaxation between responses becomes incomplete and eventually the force can become smoothly sustained in fused tetanus.

Silent motor units in B cannot generate the repeated active response. An infinitely long sarcomere in C is neither physiological nor a requirement for summation, and cutting the optic nerve in D is unrelated. Keep temporal summation separate from recruitment: summation changes the timing of activity in already active fibers, whereas recruitment adds more motor units to the contraction.

## w06-mc22

## w06-mc23

As the demand for force rises, additional and progressively larger motor units are brought into activity. The nervous system can simultaneously increase firing rates in already recruited units, so recruitment and frequency coding can cooperate.

## w06-mc24

## w06-mc25

**A gives the correct structural hierarchy:** a whole muscle contains fascicles; each fascicle contains muscle fibers; each fiber contains myofibrils; and each myofibril contains sarcomeres arranged in series. A skeletal muscle fiber is one long, multinucleated cell, not a bundle of smaller muscle cells.

Inside each sarcomere are the thick and thin filaments that interact to generate force. Thus the hierarchy connects the visible organ to the microscopic repeating machinery responsible for contraction. Connective tissue organizes the larger levels and helps transmit force, while the sarcolemma and internal membrane systems belong to the individual muscle fiber.

The other sequences mix levels in impossible orders, such as placing the entire muscle inside a myofibril or a sarcomere above a whole muscle.

## w06-mc27

## w06-sr01

Near vision requires greater refractive power because rays arriving from a nearby object diverge more strongly. The eye achieves this through **accommodation**, in which the shape of the lens changes while the retina remains in its position at the back of the eye.

For near focus, the ciliary muscle contracts. Its ring-like arrangement means that contraction reduces the pull exerted through the zonular fibers. Once that tension decreases, the lens's elasticity allows it to become rounder and more convex. The increased curvature bends incoming rays more strongly, helping bring the near object's image into focus on the retina. For distant focus, ciliary relaxation increases zonular tension and the lens becomes flatter.

Presbyopia develops as the lens becomes less flexible with age. Even when the ciliary muscle changes tension appropriately, the lens cannot increase its curvature as effectively. The range of near focusing therefore decreases. This is a limitation of optical accommodation, not necessarily a loss of photoreceptor sensitivity.

## w06-sr02

**Rods are highly sensitive** and support vision in dim light, but they do not supply the comparisons among cone classes required for normal color discrimination. Rod pathways also tend to pool information from many receptors, so their excellent sensitivity comes at the cost of spatial detail.

**Cones function best at brighter light levels** and support color vision through comparisons among cone types with different spectral sensitivities. In high-acuity regions, particularly the fovea, cones are densely packed and their signals undergo relatively little convergence. Keeping neighboring inputs distinct allows the visual system to resolve fine detail.

Cones are especially concentrated in the fovea, while rods are abundant outside the foveal center and are absent from its very center. Neither receptor type should be described as occupying only one absolute half of the retina: the distinction is a pattern of density and specialization.

More pooling helps detect weak light but obscures the exact receptor of origin, whereas low convergence supports fine spatial discrimination under conditions in which individual cone signals are sufficiently strong.

## w06-sr03

In darkness, cGMP keeps outer-segment cyclic-nucleotide-gated cation channels open. Their inward current helps keep the rod relatively depolarized, supporting ongoing calcium entry at the synaptic terminal and tonic glutamate release.

A photon is absorbed by the retinal component of rhodopsin, producing a conformational change that activates the pigment. Activated rhodopsin stimulates the G protein transducin, which activates phosphodiesterase. Phosphodiesterase breaks down cGMP, so cGMP concentration falls and the cation channels close.

The reduction in inward positive current, while outward potassium current continues, makes the rod membrane more negative: it **hyperpolarizes**. This reduces activation of synaptic voltage-gated calcium channels and therefore reduces glutamate release. The receptor response is graded rather than an action potential of fixed amplitude.

Retinal circuits then interpret the change in glutamate. ON and OFF pathways respond differently, so reduced rod transmitter release should not be summarized as every downstream neuron becoming less active. Retinal ganglion cells ultimately encode processed visual information in spikes.

## w06-sr04

The major image-forming pathway begins with photoreceptors signalling through retinal circuits to **ganglion cells**. Ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm, fibers from each nasal hemiretina cross, while fibers from each temporal hemiretina remain on the same side. Beyond the chiasm, these axons form the optic tracts.

The major pathway then relays in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and continues through optic radiations to primary visual cortex. Additional retinal projections support functions such as reflexes, but they are not substitutes for this principal conscious-vision pathway.

Because the eye reverses the image optically, the left visual field is represented on complementary retinal halves in the two eyes. Crossing only the nasal fibers brings those signals together in the right hemisphere. The reverse arrangement applies to the right visual field and left hemisphere.

Avoid saying that all optic fibers cross or that one hemisphere receives the complete visual world from only one eye.

## w06-sr05

An action potential reaches the motor-neuron terminal and opens presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels. The resulting calcium entry triggers vesicle fusion and release of **acetylcholine (ACh)** into the synaptic cleft. ACh diffuses to the motor end plate, where it binds nicotinic receptors on the muscle membrane.

These receptors are ligand-gated cation channels. At the resting muscle potential, their opening produces a net inward positive current and a local **end-plate potential**. That potential is graded: its size depends on the effective synaptic current. If it depolarizes adjacent membrane to threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and generate the muscle action potential.

The muscle action potential propagates along the sarcolemma and T-tubules, providing the excitation needed for later calcium release and contraction. Acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh in the cleft, helping terminate the synaptic signal; transmitter removal should not be confused with the subsequent removal of calcium from muscle cytosol.

The nerve's action potential does not simply jump across the cleft unchanged.

## w06-sr06

A sarcomere extends from one Z disc to the next. Thin filaments contain **actin** and anchor at the Z discs, extending inward toward the center. Thick filaments contain **myosin** and occupy the central region around the M line. Myosin heads bind actin and use ATP-dependent cycling to generate force.

The thin filament also carries the regulatory proteins **troponin and tropomyosin**. At low cytosolic calcium, tropomyosin restricts myosin's access to actin-binding sites. Calcium binding to troponin changes the regulatory complex and shifts tropomyosin, allowing effective cross-bridge formation.

**Titin** is a large elastic protein extending between the Z-disc region and the central thick-filament region. It helps position and stabilize myosin and contributes passive elasticity. **Nebulin** lies along the thin filament and supports its organization and alignment. Neither should be confused with the ATPase motor or the main calcium-sensitive switch.

Filament overlap determines how many cross-bridges can operate, regulatory proteins determine whether access is permitted, and structural proteins help maintain alignment and elastic behavior.

## w06-sr07

Begin with calcium rising in the muscle cytosol. **Ca²⁺ binds troponin**, shifting tropomyosin and making actin sites accessible. An energized myosin head carrying ADP and inorganic phosphate can then interact with actin. Release of phosphate is associated with the force-generating transition, in which the head changes conformation and pulls relative to the thin filament. ADP is subsequently released.

A new ATP molecule binds to myosin and causes **detachment from actin**. Hydrolysis of that ATP then re-cocks the head into an energized state. If calcium remains elevated and an actin site is available, the head can attach again and repeat the cycle. Many heads cycle asynchronously, producing sustained force at the filament level.

Calcium controls access to actin through the regulatory proteins; ATP supports myosin cycling and is specifically necessary for detachment. ATP is also used by SERCA to remove calcium during relaxation, but that is a separate use from the cross-bridge cycle itself.

## w06-sr08

Excitation–contraction coupling connects an action potential in the muscle membrane with activation of the contractile proteins. The muscle action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and enters **T-tubules**, carrying the voltage change deep into the fiber. At triads, voltage sensors in the T-tubule membrane lie next to calcium-release channels in the SR.

Depolarization changes the conformation of the dihydropyridine receptor, the skeletal-muscle voltage sensor. Its mechanical coupling to the ryanodine receptor opens the SR release pathway. Stored calcium enters the cytosol and binds troponin, causing tropomyosin to move so that myosin can bind actin. Cross-bridge cycling then generates force, provided ATP is available.

The skeletal mechanism should be distinguished from cardiac calcium-induced calcium release. In skeletal muscle, the immediate coupling does not depend on extracellular calcium first entering through the L-type channel to trigger SR release.

The action potential does not pull the filaments directly. When excitation ceases, release declines and SERCA lowers cytosolic calcium, reversing the regulatory activation and allowing relaxation.

## w06-sr09

When motor-neuron firing stops, new action potentials no longer arrive to trigger ACh release. Acetylcholinesterase removes ACh from the synaptic cleft, the end-plate depolarization subsides, and no further muscle action potentials are initiated under normal conditions. Without continuing excitation, SR calcium-release channels no longer sustain the activation signal.

Calcium already present in the cytosol must still be removed. **SERCA uses ATP to pump Ca²⁺ back into the SR**, lowering free cytosolic calcium. Calcium then dissociates from troponin, and the troponin–tropomyosin complex returns toward the state that restricts myosin binding to actin.

As existing cross-bridges detach through ATP binding and fewer new force-generating attachments form, active tension declines. Elastic structures and external loads help determine how the muscle's length changes as it relaxes.

Relaxation requires energy for calcium pumping and cross-bridge detachment; it is not a state achieved just by withdrawing ATP from the cell.

## w06-sr10

There are two different ways to increase whole-muscle force: change the activity of fibers already contracting, or add more fibers to the contraction. **Temporal summation** uses the first strategy. A new action potential arrives before the mechanical response to the previous one has fully ended, producing additional calcium release and greater force while some activation persists.

As stimulation frequency rises, less relaxation occurs between responses. The force may show oscillations during unfused tetanus and become relatively smooth during fused tetanus. The individual action potentials remain separate; it is the longer-lasting calcium-dependent mechanical response that sums.

**Motor-unit recruitment** uses the second strategy. Activating additional motor neurons brings their associated muscle fibers into the response. According to the size principle, smaller, lower-threshold units are generally recruited first, followed by larger units as drive and force requirements increase.

Neither requires individual muscle action potentials to become larger, and activating every unit simultaneously would eliminate much of the graded control needed for precise movements.

## w06-sr11

Active muscle tension depends partly on how many myosin heads can form productive cross-bridges with actin at the fiber's initial length. Near an **optimal sarcomere length**, thick and thin filaments overlap in a configuration that permits many effective interactions, so maximal active force is relatively high.

At long lengths, overlap decreases. Even with normal calcium activation and ATP availability, some myosin heads cannot reach actin, reducing the number of productive cross-bridges. With sufficiently little overlap, active force becomes very small. At excessively short lengths, interference among thin filaments and between filaments and the sarcomere boundaries also reduces useful force generation.

This explanation concerns **active tension generated by cross-bridges**. Passive tension from elastic elements, including titin and connective tissue, tends to increase as a muscle is stretched. Total measured tension can therefore include a growing passive component even when active tension is decreasing. A graph must be interpreted according to which of these quantities it shows.

## w06-sr12

Locate the toxin's action precisely: it prevents **calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum**, while the motor neuron and neuromuscular junction remain intact. An arriving motor-neuron action potential can therefore still trigger presynaptic calcium entry, ACh release, nicotinic-receptor activation, and an end-plate potential. A muscle action potential can also be generated and propagate along the sarcolemma and T-tubules.

The interruption occurs after electrical excitation reaches the release machinery. Without adequate SR calcium release, cytosolic Ca²⁺ does not rise enough to bind and activate troponin normally. Tropomyosin continues to restrict access to actin sites, so cross-bridge recruitment and force are greatly reduced or absent, depending on the extent of the block.

ATP can support cycling only when the contractile proteins are appropriately activated; it cannot substitute for the calcium-sensitive regulatory switch.

Compare it with nicotinic-receptor blockade: that earlier interruption can prevent the muscle action potential itself, whereas the SR-release block allows excitation but disrupts its conversion into force.
