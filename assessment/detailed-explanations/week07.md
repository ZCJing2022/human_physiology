## w07-mc01

**B is the intended answer in the lecture's comparison of cardiac muscle-cell types.** Most cardiac muscle cells are working, force-producing myocytes, whereas a much smaller specialized population initiates or conducts the rhythmic electrical activity that coordinates their contraction. Working myocytes contain organized contractile machinery and generate the force needed to eject blood.

Autorhythmic cells are crucial even though they are not the main force-producing population. They can depolarize spontaneously and set the timing of activation. The importance of a cell type therefore should not be confused with its abundance or its direct contribution to force.

## w07-mc02

**Gap junctions provide low-resistance electrical connections between adjacent cardiac cells**, so A is correct. Ions can pass through these channels from one cell's cytoplasm to the next. Depolarizing current from an activated myocyte can therefore help bring a neighboring myocyte to threshold, allowing excitation to spread through the tissue.

This electrical coupling helps a region of myocardium act as a **functional syncytium**: the cells remain separate anatomically but are coordinated electrically. Intercalated discs also contain mechanical junctions that help prevent cells from pulling apart during contraction. Electrical coupling and mechanical attachment are related needs but are performed by different junctional components.

Gap junctions are not calcium-storage compartments, so B confuses them with the sarcoplasmic reticulum. They permit rather than prevent selected ion movement, contradicting C. Hemoglobin synthesis in D is unrelated to their function. The central link is **cell-to-cell current flow → coordinated depolarization → coordinated contraction**, rather than every cardiac myocyte requiring an individual motor-neuron command.

## w07-mc03

In a ventricular myocyte, depolarization opens L-type calcium channels in the surface and T-tubule membranes. The entering calcium activates nearby ryanodine receptors in the SR, producing additional calcium release into the cytosol. **A is correct: this is calcium-induced calcium release.**

The extracellular calcium acts as a trigger and also contributes to the calcium signal, but the SR supplies a substantial additional amount. The resulting rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ activates troponin, permits actin–myosin interaction, and produces force. This sequence explains why calcium-channel activity strongly influences cardiac contractility.

Nicotinic ACh receptors in B belong to skeletal neuromuscular transmission, not the cardiac release mechanism. Permanent closure of every potassium channel is neither required nor compatible with normal repolarization. Calcium does not become the iron in hemoglobin. Contrast this mechanism with skeletal muscle, where the immediate SR-release trigger is mechanical coupling to a voltage sensor rather than a requirement for incoming calcium to trigger release.

## w07-mc04

Cardiac relaxation requires a fall in cytosolic Ca²⁺ so that calcium leaves troponin and contractile activation declines. **A identifies two major mechanisms:** SERCA returns calcium to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, while the sodium–calcium exchanger helps move calcium out of the cell across its surface membrane.

These processes have different energy relationships. SERCA directly uses ATP to pump calcium into the SR. The sodium–calcium exchanger normally uses the inward sodium electrochemical gradient to support calcium extrusion; that sodium gradient is maintained by the sodium–potassium ATPase. Relaxation therefore depends on functioning transport systems and adequate energy supply, not simply on the passage of time.

Opening nicotinic receptors, activating rhodopsin, and aggregating platelets concern other systems and do not describe the major route for removing cardiac cytosolic calcium. It is also incorrect to imagine that all calcium must leave the body or the myocyte: much is **recycled into the SR for subsequent beats**, while the amount that entered from outside must be balanced by extrusion.

## w07-mc05

**B correctly traces the pulmonary circuit:** blood leaves the right ventricle through the pulmonary valve and pulmonary artery, passes through lung vessels and pulmonary capillaries, returns through pulmonary veins to the left atrium, and enters the left ventricle through the mitral valve.

The naming rule is direction relative to the heart. An **artery carries blood away from the heart**, and a **vein returns blood toward it**. Oxygen content is not the defining rule. Pulmonary arteries normally carry relatively deoxygenated blood to the lungs, while pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood. This is the familiar exception to the misleading shortcut that all arteries are oxygenated and all veins are deoxygenated.

## w07-mc06

Heart valves respond mainly to **pressure differences across them**, making B correct. When the pressure on the inflow side exceeds the pressure on the outflow side in the permitted direction, the valve is pushed open. When the gradient reverses, the valve closes and prevents substantial backward flow under normal conditions.

The valves do not actively contract to open themselves, and they are not individually commanded by motor neurons. Papillary muscles and chordae tendineae stabilize the atrioventricular valves during ventricular contraction, helping prevent prolapse; they do not pull the valves open to start filling.

Platelet activation concerns hemostasis rather than the normal beat-to-beat positioning of valves. The pressure rule is particularly useful for interpreting the cardiac cycle: the mitral valve opens when atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure, while the aortic valve opens when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure. **Electrical activity changes muscle contraction; contraction changes pressure; pressure changes valve position.** Keeping those stages separate prevents many timing errors.

## w07-mc16

An ECG records **electrical potential differences between electrodes on the body surface**, generated by the heart's coordinated electrical activity. **A is correct.** The tracing reflects the summed activity of many cells and its orientation relative to the recording electrodes, not the membrane potential of one isolated cardiac cell.

## w07-mc20

$$SV = EDV - ESV = 130\;\text{mL} - 50\;\text{mL} = 80\;\text{mL/beat}.$$

**B is correct.** A simple check is to imagine a chamber containing 130 mL before ejection and 50 mL afterward: the missing 80 mL is what left the chamber during that beat.

## w07-mc21

Cardiac output is the volume pumped by one ventricle **per minute**. Heart rate tells you how many beats occur in that minute, and stroke volume tells you how much is ejected per beat. Multiply them so that the beat units cancel:

$$CO = HR\times SV = 75\;\frac{\text{beats}}{\text{min}}\times80\;\frac{\text{mL}}{\text{beat}} = 6000\;\text{mL/min} = 6.0\;\text{L/min}.$$

**B is correct.** Converting from milliliters to liters requires dividing by 1000. The answers 0.6 and 60 L/min represent tenfold scale errors relative to the given values. Adding 75 and 80 is not meaningful because heart rate and stroke volume have different units; it cannot produce a valid flow measurement.

As a reasonableness check, about 80 mL on each of 75 beats should produce several thousand milliliters in one minute. Cardiac output is conventionally the output of either ventricle in a steady circulation, not the sum of right- and left-ventricular outputs.

## w07-mc22

## w07-sr01

Compare the two cell types along each dimension requested. Working cardiac myocytes are relatively short and branched, usually with one central nucleus, whereas skeletal muscle fibers are long, cylindrical, multinucleated cells. Both contain organized sarcomeres and generate force through regulated actin–myosin interaction.

Cardiac cells are connected through intercalated discs, including gap junctions that allow electrical current to spread between neighboring cells. Skeletal fibers are not normally activated through such cell-to-cell electrical coupling; each fiber receives neuromuscular input as part of a motor unit. This difference supports coordinated cardiac excitation while allowing skeletal muscles to grade force by selective recruitment.

Their immediate calcium-release mechanisms also differ. In ventricular myocytes, calcium entering through L-type channels triggers additional SR release. In skeletal muscle, the T-tubule voltage sensor is mechanically coupled to the SR release channel, so extracellular calcium entry is not the required immediate trigger in the same way. Both ultimately use calcium binding to troponin and ATP-dependent cross-bridge cycling.

Finally, the long cardiac action potential and refractory period prevent ordinary tetanic summation, preserving time for filling between beats. Skeletal fibers can summate twitches because their electrical refractory period is much shorter than their mechanical response. A strong comparison explains **how cell structure, electrical organization, and calcium handling fit the different jobs of pumping and controlled movement**.

## w07-sr02

An action potential depolarizes the ventricular myocyte's surface and T-tubule membranes, opening **L-type calcium channels**. Calcium enters from the extracellular fluid and activates nearby ryanodine receptors in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. These channels release a larger additional quantity of stored calcium into the cytosol, a process called calcium-induced calcium release.

The rise in cytosolic calcium activates troponin, shifting tropomyosin and permitting myosin to interact with actin. ATP-dependent cross-bridge cycling then generates force. The extracellular entry is therefore both a contribution to the calcium balance and a trigger for amplifying the intracellular calcium signal; it should not be described as the only calcium used for contraction.

Relaxation follows as calcium entry and release decline and removal mechanisms lower cytosolic calcium. SERCA pumps calcium back into the SR, while sodium–calcium exchange and other membrane transport mechanisms contribute to extrusion. Calcium leaves troponin, regulatory inhibition returns, and active tension decreases.

A complete answer connects **electrical excitation, trigger calcium, SR amplification, myofilament activation, and removal of calcium**. Contrast the cardiac trigger with skeletal mechanical coupling, rather than assuming that identical names for T-tubules and SR imply identical coupling mechanisms. This distinction also explains why changing cardiac L-type calcium entry can strongly alter contraction strength.

## w07-sr04

A working ventricular myocyte normally begins at a relatively stable negative resting potential. During **phase 0**, rapid opening of voltage-gated sodium channels produces the steep depolarizing upstroke. Sodium-channel inactivation and transient outward potassium movement contribute to the early repolarizing notch, or phase 1.

During **phase 2**, inward calcium current through L-type channels is balanced against outward potassium currents, producing the plateau. A flat-looking voltage does not imply that ion movement has stopped; opposing currents approximately balance. As calcium current declines and outward potassium current predominates, **phase 3** returns the membrane toward its resting state. Phase 4 is the resting interval in the working ventricular cell, not the spontaneous pacemaker drift of an SA-node cell.

The duration has a mechanical consequence. The long depolarization keeps the cell refractory through much of its contraction, so a new propagated action potential cannot be triggered early enough to produce ordinary tetanic summation. The ventricle can therefore relax and refill between effective beats.

The plateau also supports the calcium entry that triggers SR calcium release and contraction. A strong answer links **channel behavior to the voltage trace and then to pumping function**. Do not assign the SA node's calcium-dependent upstroke or unstable diastolic potential to a normal working ventricular myocyte.

## w07-sr05

SA nodal cells do not remain at a stable resting potential between beats. After repolarization, changing membrane currents create a **slow diastolic depolarization** that moves the membrane back toward threshold. Funny current through HCN channels contributes net inward current at negative voltages, while changing calcium and potassium currents also participate.

When threshold is reached, calcium-channel opening produces the nodal action-potential upstroke. Potassium efflux then contributes to repolarization, and the cycle begins again. This differs from the fast sodium-dependent upstroke of a working ventricular myocyte. The SA node can therefore generate a rhythm without requiring a new motor-neuron signal for every beat.

Sympathetic β1 signalling increases cAMP-related effects and promotes faster pacemaker depolarization, so threshold is reached sooner and heart rate rises. Parasympathetic ACh acting at M2 receptors reduces the depolarizing drive and increases potassium conductance, slowing the approach to threshold and often making the starting voltage more negative. Heart rate falls.

The explanation should connect autonomic action to **the time needed to reach the next threshold**, not merely say that sympathetic means faster. A steeper depolarizing slope or a less negative starting point can shorten the interval, whereas a flatter slope or more negative starting point can lengthen it. Both change timing rather than the definition of an individual heartbeat.

## w07-sr06

Excitation normally begins in the **SA node** and spreads through the atrial myocardium, producing atrial depolarization and then atrial contraction. The signal reaches the **AV node**, where conduction slows. It then travels through the **bundle of His**, the **right and left bundle branches**, and **Purkinje fibers** before spreading through the ventricular myocardium.

The AV nodal delay separates atrial activation from the start of coordinated ventricular activation. This gives atrial contraction time to contribute to ventricular filling before ventricular pressure rises and closes the AV valves. Most filling is passive at rest, but the timing of the final atrial contribution remains functionally important.

After the delay, rapid distribution through the His–Purkinje system allows ventricular regions to activate in an organized pattern rather than one slow, uncoordinated contraction. The heart's fibrous separation between atria and ventricles helps make the normal specialized conduction route important for the timing of communication between the chambers.

A complete response therefore explains two different timing functions: **slow conduction at the AV node coordinates the chambers, while rapid conduction through ventricular pathways coordinates the ventricular pump**. Do not confuse this electrical route with the path blood takes. Electrical activation precedes contraction, which then changes pressures and valve positions to direct blood flow.

## w07-sr07

The **P wave** represents atrial depolarization. Atrial contraction follows, producing a small increase in atrial pressure and contributing the final portion of ventricular filling. The ECG wave itself does not measure that pressure rise or the force of contraction.

The **QRS complex** represents ventricular depolarization. Ventricular contraction follows, raising ventricular pressure. Once it exceeds atrial pressure, AV valves close; after pressure exceeds outflow-artery pressure, semilunar valves open and ejection begins. Atrial repolarization usually occurs around the QRS period but is obscured by the larger ventricular signal.

The **T wave** represents ventricular repolarization. It occurs as the electrical state recovers and the ventricle moves toward relaxation. Calcium removal and declining force allow ventricular pressure to fall, leading eventually to semilunar closure and, later, AV-valve opening for filling.

Do not make the mapping more rigid than physiology allows. An ECG landmark does not directly command a valve, and electrical recovery is not identical to an instantaneous mechanical event. A useful answer links **electrical activation or recovery → calcium-dependent mechanical change → pressure-dependent blood flow and valve movement**. This causal sequence lets you use the ECG to orient a Wiggers diagram without mistaking an electrical recording for a pressure, force, or volume measurement.

## w07-sr08

Build the Wiggers diagram on one shared time axis, aligning the ECG, left atrial pressure, left ventricular pressure, aortic pressure, ventricular volume, valve events, and heart sounds. Reconstruct the sequence from pressure differences rather than memorizing an isolated picture.

During **ventricular filling**, the mitral valve is open and the aortic valve closed. Ventricular volume rises while ventricular pressure remains relatively low. Near the end of filling, the P wave precedes atrial contraction, which produces a small atrial-pressure rise and adds the final filling contribution. Ventricular volume reaches **EDV**.

The QRS complex marks ventricular depolarization. Contraction follows and ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, closing the mitral valve; this is associated with **S1**. During **isovolumetric contraction**, both valves are closed, pressure rises, and volume remains at EDV. When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens and **ejection** begins. Ventricular volume falls, while ventricular and aortic pressures initially rise and then decline. The atrium meanwhile continues receiving pulmonary venous blood behind its closed outlet, contributing to a later rise in atrial pressure.

The T wave represents ventricular repolarization near the later part of the systolic sequence. As ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure, the aortic valve closes, associated with **S2** and the aortic pressure notch. Volume has reached **ESV**, not zero. During **isovolumetric relaxation**, both valves are closed and pressure falls at constant ESV.

Finally, ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, opening the mitral valve and beginning the next filling phase. Aortic pressure declines more gradually during diastole because elastic recoil sustains arterial pressure. Check your diagram by asking whether volume changes only when an appropriate valve is open, whether S1 and S2 accompany closure rather than normal opening, and whether electrical events precede the mechanical changes they initiate.

## w07-sr09

Both isovolumetric phases occur when **the ventricular inlet and outlet valves are closed**. With no significant inflow or outflow in the normal idealized cycle, the blood volume in the chamber stays approximately constant even though myocardial tension and chamber pressure change.

During isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure has just risen above atrial pressure, closing the AV valve, but remains below arterial pressure, so the semilunar valve has not yet opened. The ventricle contains its EDV while pressure rises. This phase ends when the ventricular-to-arterial pressure gradient becomes sufficient to open the outlet valve and initiate ejection.

During isovolumetric relaxation, the semilunar valve has closed after ejection, but ventricular pressure is still above atrial pressure, keeping the AV valve closed. Volume remains at ESV while pressure falls. Once ventricular pressure drops below atrial pressure, the inlet valve opens and filling resumes.

The two phases therefore differ in **direction of pressure change and the volume at which they occur**: rising pressure at EDV versus falling pressure at ESV. Constant volume does not imply that the muscle is inactive or that pressure is constant. It also does not mean the chamber is empty; the ventricle normally retains a substantial volume after ejection.

## w07-sr10

Define the variables by placing them in the cardiac cycle. **EDV** is the ventricular volume at the end of filling, just before contraction. **ESV** is the volume remaining after ejection. **SV**, or stroke volume, is the amount ejected during one beat, so it is the difference between those two chamber volumes:

$$SV = EDV - ESV.$$

**HR**, or heart rate, is the number of beats per minute. **CO**, or cardiac output, is the volume pumped by one ventricle per minute. Multiplying volume per beat by beats per minute gives:

$$CO = HR\times SV.$$

For a concrete example, EDV of 130 mL and ESV of 50 mL produce SV of 80 mL/beat. At 75 beats/min, CO is 6000 mL/min, or 6.0 L/min. The units show why subtraction is appropriate for the two chamber volumes and multiplication is appropriate for the rate calculation.

These definitions also support predictions. More filling can raise EDV; stronger ejection at comparable filling can lower ESV; either can increase SV. A rate change affects CO through the second equation. Do not confuse the volume left behind with the volume ejected, or add the outputs of the two ventricles when stating ordinary cardiac output.

## w07-sr11

The **Frank–Starling relationship** is an intrinsic, length-dependent response to filling. More venous return generally increases EDV and myocardial stretch before contraction. Within the normal operating range, this greater initial length increases activation and force, allowing a larger stroke volume. It helps the heart match its output to the blood arriving at it without requiring a new sympathetic command.

**Positive inotropy** changes the contractile state at a given preload. For example, sympathetic β1 signalling enhances calcium entry and SR calcium cycling. A ventricle starting at a comparable EDV and ejecting against a comparable afterload can then develop more force and eject more completely, often lowering ESV and increasing SV.

On a cardiac-function graph, a change in filling at a fixed contractile state is represented by movement along a Frank–Starling curve. A change in contractility shifts the relationship, so a different output is possible at the same preload. This graphical distinction makes clear why two changes that both raise SV are not the same mechanism.

A strong explanation therefore compares **more initial stretch** with **greater contractile activation at the same initial stretch**. Do not label every increase in force as positive inotropy, and do not assume that stretching indefinitely improves pumping: the Frank–Starling prediction is limited to an appropriate physiological range and loading conditions.

## w07-sr12

A β1-adrenergic agonist can act on both pacemaker tissue and working myocardium, so separate its **chronotropic** and **inotropic** effects before predicting output. In the SA node, increased β1 signalling promotes a faster approach to threshold, increasing heart rate. In ventricular myocytes, enhanced calcium entry and SR calcium cycling increase contractility.

At comparable EDV and afterload, stronger ventricular contraction tends to eject more blood and leave a smaller **ESV**. From SV = EDV − ESV, a lower ESV at the same starting EDV means a larger **SV**. Combining a higher heart rate with a larger stroke volume through CO = HR × SV gives the expected increase in **CO**.

The prediction contains assumptions. Very high heart rates can shorten filling time, and changes in loading conditions or reflex responses can modify the size of the observed effect. The question is asking for the direct, usual course-level β1 pattern, not a claim that every possible dose and physiological state produces unlimited increases in output.

A complete response traces **receptor action → altered pacemaker timing and calcium handling → changes in ESV and SV → cardiac output**. It should not confuse the inotropic effect with Frank–Starling: the agonist can strengthen contraction at the same preload, rather than requiring increased filling as the initiating cause.
