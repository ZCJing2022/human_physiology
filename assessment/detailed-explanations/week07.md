# Week 07 explanation narratives

## w07-mc01

**B is the intended answer in the lecture's comparison of cardiac muscle-cell types.** Most cardiac muscle cells are working, force-producing myocytes, whereas a much smaller specialized population initiates or conducts the rhythmic electrical activity that coordinates their contraction. Working myocytes contain organized contractile machinery and generate the force needed to eject blood.

Autorhythmic cells are crucial even though they are not the main force-producing population. They can depolarize spontaneously and set the timing of activation.

Sensory neurons and erythrocytes do not constitute the contracting myocardium. An important scope qualification is that this teaching comparison concerns **cardiac muscle cells**, not a numerical census of every cell in heart tissue. The heart also contains endothelial cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, and other nonmyocytes.

## w07-mc02

**Gap junctions provide low-resistance electrical connections between adjacent cardiac cells**, so A is correct. Ions can pass through these channels from one cell's cytoplasm to the next. Depolarizing current from an activated myocyte can therefore help bring a neighboring myocyte to threshold, allowing excitation to spread through the tissue.

This electrical coupling helps a region of myocardium act as a **functional syncytium**: the cells remain separate anatomically but are coordinated electrically. Intercalated discs also contain mechanical junctions that help prevent cells from pulling apart during contraction.

Gap junctions are not calcium-storage compartments, so B confuses them with the sarcoplasmic reticulum. They permit rather than prevent selected ion movement, contradicting C. Hemoglobin synthesis in D is unrelated to their function.

## w07-mc03

In a ventricular myocyte, depolarization opens L-type calcium channels in the surface and T-tubule membranes. The entering calcium activates nearby ryanodine receptors in the SR, producing additional calcium release into the cytosol. **A is correct: this is calcium-induced calcium release.**

The resulting rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ activates troponin, permits actin–myosin interaction, and produces force.

Nicotinic ACh receptors in B belong to skeletal neuromuscular transmission, not the cardiac release mechanism. Permanent closure of every potassium channel is neither required nor compatible with normal repolarization. Calcium does not become the iron in hemoglobin. Contrast this mechanism with skeletal muscle, where the immediate SR-release trigger is mechanical coupling to a voltage sensor rather than a requirement for incoming calcium to trigger release.

## w07-mc04

Cardiac relaxation requires a fall in cytosolic Ca²⁺ so that calcium leaves troponin and contractile activation declines. **A identifies two major mechanisms:** SERCA returns calcium to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, while the sodium–calcium exchanger helps move calcium out of the cell across its surface membrane.

SERCA directly uses ATP to pump calcium into the SR. The sodium–calcium exchanger normally uses the inward sodium electrochemical gradient to support calcium extrusion; that sodium gradient is maintained by the sodium–potassium ATPase.

Opening nicotinic receptors, activating rhodopsin, and aggregating platelets concern other systems and do not describe the major route for removing cardiac cytosolic calcium. It is also incorrect to imagine that all calcium must leave the body or the myocyte: much is **recycled into the SR for subsequent beats**, while the amount that entered from outside must be balanced by extrusion.

## w07-mc05

**B correctly traces the pulmonary circuit:** blood leaves the right ventricle through the pulmonary valve and pulmonary artery, passes through lung vessels and pulmonary capillaries, returns through pulmonary veins to the left atrium, and enters the left ventricle through the mitral valve.

An **artery carries blood away from the heart**, and a **vein returns blood toward it**. Oxygen content is not the defining rule. Pulmonary arteries normally carry relatively deoxygenated blood to the lungs, while pulmonary veins return oxygenated blood.

The aorta belongs to the outlet of the left ventricle, not the immediate route from the right ventricle to the lungs. The venae cavae return systemic blood to the right atrium. The incorrect sequences mix systemic and pulmonary structures or reverse the direction of flow.

## w07-mc06

Heart valves respond mainly to **pressure differences across them**, making B correct. When the pressure on the inflow side exceeds the pressure on the outflow side in the permitted direction, the valve is pushed open. When the gradient reverses, the valve closes and prevents substantial backward flow under normal conditions.

The valves do not actively contract to open themselves, and they are not individually commanded by motor neurons. Papillary muscles and chordae tendineae stabilize the atrioventricular valves during ventricular contraction, helping prevent prolapse; they do not pull the valves open to start filling.

Platelet activation concerns hemostasis rather than the normal beat-to-beat positioning of valves. During the cardiac cycle, the mitral valve opens when atrial pressure exceeds ventricular pressure; the aortic valve opens when ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure.

## w07-mc07

## w07-mc08

A second contraction therefore cannot be triggered early enough to build the sustained summation characteristic of skeletal-muscle tetanus.

## w07-mc09

The sinoatrial node normally acts as the dominant pacemaker because its cells reach threshold spontaneously **more frequently than other potential pacemakers**, making B correct. Their impulses spread to downstream tissues before those tissues would normally fire at their own slower intrinsic rates, thereby setting the rhythm of the whole system.

Other cardiac tissues can possess automaticity and may generate escape rhythms if normal pacing or conduction fails.

## w07-mc10

The funny current, written **I_f**, contributes to the slow spontaneous depolarization between action potentials in pacemaker cells, so A is correct. It flows through HCN channels, which conduct both sodium and potassium. At the relatively negative voltages encountered during pacemaker depolarization, their net current is inward and helps move the membrane toward threshold.

These channels are unusual because hyperpolarization promotes their opening. Pacemaker activity also depends on changing calcium and potassium currents; funny current is an important contributor, not the only mechanism involved.

The ventricular plateau in B is a different phase in a different cell type and is strongly associated with L-type calcium current balanced against outward current. Clot formation and skeletal tetanus are unrelated.

## w07-mc11

**B gives the normal order of the specialized cardiac conduction pathway.** The SA node initiates excitation, which spreads through the atria and reaches the AV node. After the AV nodal delay, excitation passes through the bundle of His, the right and left bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers, which distribute it rapidly through the ventricular myocardium.

Atrial activation precedes ventricular activation, allowing the atria to contribute to ventricular filling. Rapid ventricular distribution then supports coordinated contraction rather than a slow, disorganized wave through the pumping chamber.

The other sequences reverse the normal origin or omit the required order. Electrical spread does not itself describe blood movement through the chambers: **conduction organizes contraction, and contraction then generates the pressure gradients that move blood**.

## w07-mc12

**The AV nodal delay allows atrial contraction to contribute to ventricular filling before ventricular contraction begins**, so A is correct. After the SA node activates the atria, conduction slows through the AV node.

Most ventricular filling at rest is passive, so the delay should not be described as the reason all filling occurs. Its significance is that it coordinates the final atrial contribution with the onset of ventricular systole, rather than allowing the two chambers to contract together in a way that impairs transfer.

The AV node does not prevent the atria from depolarizing: atrial depolarization has already occurred. Valve positions follow pressure gradients, not a direct instruction from the AV node, so C is wrong. The delay also does not stop SA-node pacemaking.

## w07-mc13

**The P wave represents atrial depolarization**, making A correct. It is part of a surface recording of the heart's electrical activity. After atrial cells depolarize, their excitation–contraction coupling raises cytosolic calcium and the atria contract, contributing to ventricular filling.

The sequence is therefore electrical activation first and mechanical contraction afterward. The P wave is not a direct measure of atrial force, even though its timing is associated with the atrial contraction that follows.

Ventricular depolarization corresponds to the QRS complex, while ventricular repolarization corresponds to the T wave. Those assignments eliminate B and C. When using an ECG beside a Wiggers diagram, use the P wave as a timing reference for atrial activation, then separately follow pressure and volume changes.

## w07-mc14

**The QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization**, so B is correct. A large mass of ventricular myocardium is being electrically activated, producing a prominent surface potential pattern. Excitation–contraction coupling follows this activation, leading to ventricular force development and a rise in chamber pressure.

Atrial repolarization occurs around the same period but is usually obscured by the much larger ventricular electrical signal. The absence of a separately obvious atrial repolarization wave does not mean the atria fail to repolarize.

Atrial depolarization is represented by the P wave, and ventricular repolarization by the T wave. AV valve closure occurs when rising ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure; it is a mechanical event that follows the appropriate electrical activation rather than the direct quantity recorded by the QRS complex.

## w07-mc15

**The T wave represents ventricular repolarization**, making A correct. Repolarization returns ventricular muscle-cell membranes toward their resting electrical state after the action potential. As calcium entry declines and cytosolic calcium is removed, contractile activation also decreases and the ventricle proceeds toward mechanical relaxation.

The T wave should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of ventricular filling, pressure, or the instant a valve opens. Valve events still depend on the relevant pressure differences.

Atrial depolarization in B corresponds to the P wave. SA-node activity alone is too small and too specific to define the T wave, which reflects the electrical behavior of ventricular myocardium. Ventricular filling volume in D requires a volume measurement rather than an ECG.

## w07-mc16

An ECG records **electrical potential differences between electrodes on the body surface**, generated by the heart's coordinated electrical activity. **A is correct.** The tracing reflects the summed activity of many cells and its orientation relative to the recording electrodes, not the membrane potential of one isolated cardiac cell.

Pressure, stroke volume, and valve area are mechanical or anatomical quantities. They are related to electrical activation but are not measured directly by an ECG.

The ECG supplies electrical timing, while pressure and volume curves describe mechanical events. A normal-looking waveform alone cannot establish that every mechanical variable is normal. Avoid the shortcut that a taller QRS necessarily means a stronger contraction or a larger stroke volume; the ECG is not a direct force or output meter.

## w07-mc17

During **isovolumetric ventricular contraction**, the ventricle is generating increasing pressure but has not yet begun ejecting blood. The AV valves have closed because ventricular pressure has exceeded atrial pressure. The semilunar valves remain closed because ventricular pressure has not yet exceeded the pressure in the outflow artery. Therefore **A is correct: both valve sets are closed**.

With no significant inflow or outflow in the normal idealized cycle, volume remains at end-diastolic volume (EDV) even while force develops. When ventricular pressure finally exceeds arterial pressure, the semilunar valve opens and the ejection phase begins.

All valves cannot be open during this phase, and ventricular volume does not rise rapidly because filling has ended. Pressure rises rather than falls, so D describes the wrong direction.

## w07-mc18

The aortic valve opens when the **left ventricular pressure becomes greater than the aortic pressure**, creating a forward pressure gradient from ventricle to artery. **A is correct.** Before this crossing, the ventricle is in isovolumetric contraction: pressure rises while the aortic valve remains closed and volume stays approximately constant.

Once the valve opens, blood is ejected and ventricular volume begins to fall. The opening is therefore identified from the relationship between two pressure curves, not from an ECG wave alone.

If aortic pressure exceeds ventricular pressure, as in B, the gradient favors closure rather than forward opening. Atrial pressure exceeding ventricular pressure in C is relevant to mitral-valve opening and filling. The P wave in D represents atrial depolarization and does not directly open the aortic valve.

## w07-mc19

**A is correct because the mitral valve opens when left atrial pressure exceeds left ventricular pressure.** After ejection ends, the aortic valve closes and the ventricle relaxes with both valves closed. During this isovolumetric relaxation, ventricular pressure falls while the remaining volume stays near end-systolic volume.

When ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, the forward gradient from atrium to ventricle pushes the mitral valve open. Ventricular filling then begins and volume rises. Most filling at rest occurs without requiring an atrial contraction at that exact instant; the pressure difference is sufficient to drive passive flow.

Ventricular pressure exceeding aortic pressure in B relates to aortic-valve opening and ejection. Aortic pressure being maximal does not by itself determine the mitral valve's position. The QRS complex identifies electrical activation, not a pressure-independent valve command.

## w07-mc20

Stroke volume is the volume ejected by one ventricle during one beat. **End-diastolic volume (EDV)** is the amount present just before contraction, while **end-systolic volume (ESV)** is the amount left after ejection.

$$SV = EDV - ESV = 130\;\text{mL} - 50\;\text{mL} = 80\;\text{mL/beat}.$$

**B is correct.** A simple check is to imagine a chamber containing 130 mL before ejection and 50 mL afterward: the missing 80 mL is what left the chamber during that beat.

Option A gives the volume left behind, not the amount ejected. Option C gives the starting volume and would imply complete emptying if treated as stroke volume. Option D adds the volumes even though they describe the same chamber at different times.

## w07-mc21

Cardiac output is the volume pumped by one ventricle **per minute**. Heart rate gives beats per minute; stroke volume gives milliliters per beat. Multiply them so that the beat units cancel:

$$CO = HR\times SV = 75\;\frac{\text{beats}}{\text{min}}\times80\;\frac{\text{mL}}{\text{beat}} = 6000\;\text{mL/min} = 6.0\;\text{L/min}.$$

**B is correct.** Converting from milliliters to liters requires dividing by 1000. The answers 0.6 and 60 L/min represent tenfold scale errors relative to the given values. Adding 75 and 80 is not meaningful because heart rate and stroke volume have different units; it cannot produce a valid flow measurement.

Cardiac output is conventionally the output of either ventricle in a steady circulation, not the sum of right- and left-ventricular outputs.

## w07-mc22

The **Frank–Starling mechanism** describes the intrinsic response of cardiac muscle to greater filling. Increased EDV generally increases myocardial stretch before contraction. Within the normal operating range and at a comparable contractile state and afterload, the subsequent contraction ejects a larger stroke volume. **A is correct.**

This is not merely a command from the sympathetic nervous system; the working myocardium itself responds to its initial filling condition.

Pacemaker activity does not normally stop, myocardial stretch does not fall to zero, and all valves do not remain closed. The phrase **within physiological limits** matters: it does not claim that unlimited distension produces unlimited improvement. Distinguish this filling-dependent effect from positive inotropy, which increases contractile performance at a given preload rather than requiring more filling first.

## w07-mc23

**Chronotropy refers to heart rate**, so a positive chronotropic effect increases the rate of beating and A is correct. It is primarily a statement about pacemaker timing: threshold is reached more frequently, shortening the interval between beats. Sympathetic stimulation of the SA node tends to produce this effect, whereas parasympathetic vagal input tends to slow the rate.

Option B describes positive **inotropy**, an increase in myocardial contractility at a comparable initial length or preload. Although sympathetic activity can increase both rate and contractility, the two terms name different effects and should not be treated as synonyms.

Blood viscosity in C affects resistance, and venous return in D affects filling; neither is the definition of chronotropy.

## w07-mc24

**Positive inotropy means increased myocardial contractility**, making A correct. It describes a change in the muscle's contractile state rather than an increase in force explained solely by greater initial filling. With preload and afterload comparable, a more strongly activated ventricle can eject more completely, leaving a smaller ESV and producing a larger stroke volume.

Sympathetic β1 signalling is a familiar example. It enhances calcium entry and intracellular calcium cycling, increasing the calcium available to activate the myofilaments during a beat.

A change in rate alone is chronotropy, not inotropy. Reduced blood volume primarily changes filling conditions, and sensory firing is not a definition of cardiac contractility. The key comparison is with Frank–Starling behavior: **Frank–Starling changes output through filling and length; inotropy changes contractile performance at a given preload**.

## w07-mc25

**A is correct: sympathetic β1 signalling increases the calcium signal available to activate cardiac contraction.** Through intracellular signalling that includes cAMP and protein kinase A, it enhances L-type calcium-channel activity and SR calcium handling. More calcium binds troponin, allowing stronger cross-bridge activation and greater force. Sympathetic stimulation also enhances calcium reuptake through regulation of SERCA, helping relaxation occur more rapidly and preparing the SR for subsequent beats.

## w07-mc27

## w07-sr01

Working cardiac myocytes are relatively short and branched, usually with one central nucleus, whereas skeletal muscle fibers are long, cylindrical, multinucleated cells. Both contain organized sarcomeres and generate force through regulated actin–myosin interaction.

Cardiac cells are connected through intercalated discs, including gap junctions that allow electrical current to spread between neighboring cells. Skeletal fibers are not normally activated through such cell-to-cell electrical coupling; each fiber receives neuromuscular input as part of a motor unit.

In ventricular myocytes, calcium entering through L-type channels triggers additional SR release. In skeletal muscle, the T-tubule voltage sensor is mechanically coupled to the SR release channel, so extracellular calcium entry is not the required immediate trigger in the same way. Both ultimately use calcium binding to troponin and ATP-dependent cross-bridge cycling.

Finally, the long cardiac action potential and refractory period prevent ordinary tetanic summation, preserving time for filling between beats. Skeletal fibers can summate twitches because their electrical refractory period is much shorter than their mechanical response.

## w07-sr02

An action potential depolarizes the ventricular myocyte's surface and T-tubule membranes, opening **L-type calcium channels**. Calcium enters from the extracellular fluid and activates nearby ryanodine receptors in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. These channels release a larger additional quantity of stored calcium into the cytosol, a process called calcium-induced calcium release.

The rise in cytosolic calcium activates troponin, shifting tropomyosin and permitting myosin to interact with actin. ATP-dependent cross-bridge cycling then generates force.

Relaxation follows as calcium entry and release decline and removal mechanisms lower cytosolic calcium. SERCA pumps calcium back into the SR, while sodium–calcium exchange and other membrane transport mechanisms contribute to extrusion. Calcium leaves troponin, regulatory inhibition returns, and active tension decreases.

Contrast the cardiac trigger with skeletal mechanical coupling, rather than assuming that identical names for T-tubules and SR imply identical coupling mechanisms. This distinction also explains why changing cardiac L-type calcium entry can strongly alter contraction strength.

## w07-sr03

From the vena cava it enters the **right atrium**, passes through the **tricuspid valve** into the **right ventricle**, and leaves through the **pulmonary valve** into the **pulmonary trunk and pulmonary arteries**. It then passes through progressively smaller pulmonary vessels to capillaries around the lung's gas-exchange surfaces.

After gas exchange, the cell returns through the **pulmonary veins** to the **left atrium**. It crosses the **mitral, or bicuspid, valve** into the **left ventricle** and is then ejected through the **aortic valve** into the **aorta**. From there it can enter the systemic circulation.

Chamber and valve names identify anatomical positions, while artery and vein describe direction relative to the heart. Pulmonary arteries carry blood away from the right ventricle even though that blood is relatively deoxygenated; pulmonary veins return blood even though it is oxygenated.

It should also preserve the valve locations: **AV valves lie between atria and ventricles; semilunar valves lie between ventricles and their outflow arteries**.

## w07-sr04

A working ventricular myocyte normally begins at a relatively stable negative resting potential. During **phase 0**, rapid opening of voltage-gated sodium channels produces the steep depolarizing upstroke. Sodium-channel inactivation and transient outward potassium movement contribute to the early repolarizing notch, or phase 1.

During **phase 2**, inward calcium current through L-type channels is balanced against outward potassium currents, producing the plateau. As calcium current declines and outward potassium current predominates, **phase 3** returns the membrane toward its resting state. Phase 4 is the resting interval in the working ventricular cell, not the spontaneous pacemaker drift of an SA-node cell.

The long depolarization keeps the cell refractory through much of its contraction, so a new propagated action potential cannot be triggered early enough to produce ordinary tetanic summation. The ventricle can therefore relax and refill between effective beats.

The plateau also supports the calcium entry that triggers SR calcium release and contraction. Do not assign the SA node's calcium-dependent upstroke or unstable diastolic potential to a normal working ventricular myocyte.

## w07-sr05

SA nodal cells do not remain at a stable resting potential between beats. After repolarization, changing membrane currents create a **slow diastolic depolarization** that moves the membrane back toward threshold. Funny current through HCN channels contributes net inward current at negative voltages, while changing calcium and potassium currents also participate.

When threshold is reached, calcium-channel opening produces the nodal action-potential upstroke. Potassium efflux then contributes to repolarization, and the cycle begins again. This differs from the fast sodium-dependent upstroke of a working ventricular myocyte.

Sympathetic β1 signalling increases cAMP-related effects and promotes faster pacemaker depolarization, so threshold is reached sooner and heart rate rises. Parasympathetic ACh acting at M2 receptors reduces the depolarizing drive and increases potassium conductance, slowing the approach to threshold and often making the starting voltage more negative. Heart rate falls.

A steeper depolarizing slope or a less negative starting point can shorten the interval, whereas a flatter slope or more negative starting point can lengthen it.

## w07-sr06

Excitation normally begins in the **SA node** and spreads through the atrial myocardium, producing atrial depolarization and then atrial contraction. The signal reaches the **AV node**, where conduction slows. It then travels through the **bundle of His**, the **right and left bundle branches**, and **Purkinje fibers** before spreading through the ventricular myocardium.

This gives atrial contraction time to contribute to ventricular filling before ventricular pressure rises and closes the AV valves. Most filling is passive at rest, but the timing of the final atrial contribution remains functionally important.

After the delay, rapid distribution through the His–Purkinje system allows ventricular regions to activate in an organized pattern rather than one slow, uncoordinated contraction. The heart's fibrous separation between atria and ventricles helps make the normal specialized conduction route important for the timing of communication between the chambers.

Do not confuse this electrical route with the path blood takes. Electrical activation precedes contraction, which then changes pressures and valve positions to direct blood flow.

## w07-sr07

The **P wave** represents atrial depolarization. Atrial contraction follows, producing a small increase in atrial pressure and contributing the final portion of ventricular filling. The ECG wave itself does not measure that pressure rise or the force of contraction.

The **QRS complex** represents ventricular depolarization. Ventricular contraction follows, raising ventricular pressure. Once it exceeds atrial pressure, AV valves close; after pressure exceeds outflow-artery pressure, semilunar valves open and ejection begins. Atrial repolarization usually occurs around the QRS period but is obscured by the larger ventricular signal.

The **T wave** represents ventricular repolarization. It occurs as the electrical state recovers and the ventricle moves toward relaxation. Calcium removal and declining force allow ventricular pressure to fall, leading eventually to semilunar closure and, later, AV-valve opening for filling.

Do not make the mapping more rigid than physiology allows. An ECG landmark does not directly command a valve, and electrical recovery is not identical to an instantaneous mechanical event.

## w07-sr08

Align the ECG, atrial, ventricular and aortic pressures, ventricular volume, valve events, and heart sounds on one shared time axis.

During **ventricular filling**, the mitral valve is open and the aortic valve closed. Ventricular volume rises while ventricular pressure remains relatively low. Near the end of filling, the P wave precedes atrial contraction, which produces a small atrial-pressure rise and adds the final filling contribution. Ventricular volume reaches **EDV**.

The QRS complex marks ventricular depolarization. Contraction follows and ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, closing the mitral valve; this is associated with **S1**. During **isovolumetric contraction**, both valves are closed, pressure rises, and volume remains at EDV. When ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens and **ejection** begins. Ventricular volume falls, while ventricular and aortic pressures initially rise and then decline. The atrium meanwhile continues receiving pulmonary venous blood behind its closed outlet, contributing to a later rise in atrial pressure.

The T wave represents ventricular repolarization near the later part of the systolic sequence. As ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure, the aortic valve closes, associated with **S2** and the aortic pressure notch. Volume has reached **ESV**, not zero. During **isovolumetric relaxation**, both valves are closed and pressure falls at constant ESV.

Finally, ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure, opening the mitral valve and beginning the next filling phase. Aortic pressure declines more gradually during diastole because elastic recoil sustains arterial pressure.

## w07-sr09

Both isovolumetric phases occur when **the ventricular inlet and outlet valves are closed**. With no significant inflow or outflow in the normal idealized cycle, the blood volume in the chamber stays approximately constant even though myocardial tension and chamber pressure change.

During isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure has just risen above atrial pressure, closing the AV valve, but remains below arterial pressure, so the semilunar valve has not yet opened. The ventricle contains its EDV while pressure rises. This phase ends when the ventricular-to-arterial pressure gradient becomes sufficient to open the outlet valve and initiate ejection.

During isovolumetric relaxation, the semilunar valve has closed after ejection, but ventricular pressure is still above atrial pressure, keeping the AV valve closed. Volume remains at ESV while pressure falls. Once ventricular pressure drops below atrial pressure, the inlet valve opens and filling resumes.

Constant volume does not imply that the muscle is inactive or that pressure is constant.

## w07-sr10

**EDV** is the ventricular volume at the end of filling, just before contraction. **ESV** is the volume remaining after ejection. **SV**, or stroke volume, is the amount ejected during one beat, so it is the difference between those two chamber volumes:

$$SV = EDV - ESV.$$

**HR**, or heart rate, is the number of beats per minute. **CO**, or cardiac output, is the volume pumped by one ventricle per minute. Multiplying volume per beat by beats per minute gives:

$$CO = HR\times SV.$$

For a concrete example, EDV of 130 mL and ESV of 50 mL produce SV of 80 mL/beat. At 75 beats/min, CO is 6000 mL/min, or 6.0 L/min.

More filling can raise EDV; stronger ejection at comparable filling can lower ESV; either can increase SV. A rate change affects CO through the second equation. Do not confuse the volume left behind with the volume ejected, or add the outputs of the two ventricles when stating ordinary cardiac output.

## w07-sr11

The **Frank–Starling relationship** is an intrinsic, length-dependent response to filling. More venous return generally increases EDV and myocardial stretch before contraction. Within the normal operating range, this greater initial length increases activation and force, allowing a larger stroke volume.

**Positive inotropy** changes the contractile state at a given preload. For example, sympathetic β1 signalling enhances calcium entry and SR calcium cycling. A ventricle starting at a comparable EDV and ejecting against a comparable afterload can then develop more force and eject more completely, often lowering ESV and increasing SV.

On a cardiac-function graph, a change in filling at a fixed contractile state is represented by movement along a Frank–Starling curve. A change in contractility shifts the relationship, so a different output is possible at the same preload.

Do not label every increase in force as positive inotropy, and do not assume that stretching indefinitely improves pumping: the Frank–Starling prediction is limited to an appropriate physiological range and loading conditions.

## w07-sr12

A β1-adrenergic agonist can act on both pacemaker tissue and working myocardium, so separate its **chronotropic** and **inotropic** effects before predicting output. In the SA node, increased β1 signalling promotes a faster approach to threshold, increasing heart rate. In ventricular myocytes, enhanced calcium entry and SR calcium cycling increase contractility.

At comparable EDV and afterload, stronger ventricular contraction tends to eject more blood and leave a smaller **ESV**. From SV = EDV − ESV, a lower ESV at the same starting EDV means a larger **SV**. Combining a higher heart rate with a larger stroke volume through CO = HR × SV gives the expected increase in **CO**.

Very high heart rates can shorten filling time, and changes in loading conditions or reflex responses can modify the size of the observed effect.

It should not confuse the inotropic effect with Frank–Starling: the agonist can strengthen contraction at the same preload, rather than requiring increased filling as the initiating cause.
