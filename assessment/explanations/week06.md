## w06-mc01

Trace the physical route of light rather than the route of the neural signal leaving the eye. Light first passes through the **cornea**, then the aqueous humor, through the pupil, and through the lens. It subsequently crosses the vitreous humor before reaching the retina. **B** gives the correct order among the structures listed, although the full pathway also includes the vitreous humor.

The pupil is an opening controlled by the iris, not a transparent lens that itself supplies the eye's focusing power. The cornea and lens refract light, while the retina contains the cells that begin sensory transduction. The optic nerve carries electrical output from retinal ganglion cells away from the eye; it is not a route through which incoming light reaches the lens. Keep the incoming optical path separate from the outgoing neural pathway.

## w06-mc02

Refraction occurs because light propagates at different speeds in different media. At an oblique boundary, that speed change alters the direction of the ray. The cornea and lens use this principle to bring light toward a focus on the retina, so **A** is the intended answer.

There is a useful qualification: a ray entering exactly perpendicular to a boundary can change speed without changing direction. The choice describes the bending that matters for the eye's curved refracting surfaces, rather than a claim that every ray must bend at every interface. Refraction is a physical optical event; it occurs before photoreceptor transduction and optic-nerve signaling. Motor-unit activation and conversion to sound are unrelated processes. When explaining vision, first ask how light is focused, then how retinal cells convert the focused light into neural information.

## w06-mc03

Light from a nearby object reaches the eye as relatively divergent rays and requires greater refractive power to focus on the retina. During near accommodation, the **ciliary muscle contracts**, reducing tension in the zonular fibers that normally pull on the lens. The lens's elasticity then allows it to become rounder and more convex. This makes **B** correct.

The apparent reversal is important: contraction of the ciliary muscle does not pull the lens flat. It changes the geometry of the supporting ring so that the zonules relax. For distance vision, greater zonular tension holds the lens flatter. Becoming opaque would interfere with light transmission rather than improve focusing, and accommodation does not work by moving the lens into the retina. Trace muscle contraction to zonular tension, lens curvature, and refractive power rather than memorizing only the final shape.

## w06-mc04

**Presbyopia** is the age-related reduction in the eye's ability to accommodate, especially for near objects. As the lens becomes less able to change shape, ciliary-muscle activity produces less increase in lens curvature and refractive power. **B** is therefore correct.

The difficulty is primarily with adjusting focus, not with detecting dim light or transmitting information through the optic nerve. Rod loss would concern photoreceptor function and does not define presbyopia. Auditory hair cells belong to hearing, and skeletal muscle calcium stores do not provide the lens's refracting action. Also distinguish presbyopia from myopia: presbyopia concerns reduced accommodative flexibility, whereas myopia concerns where light focuses relative to the retina in the unaccommodated eye. The key causal chain is reduced lens flexibility, reduced near-focusing power, and increased difficulty bringing close objects into sharp focus.

## w06-mc05

**Rods** are especially sensitive to low levels of light and therefore support dim-light vision, making **B** correct. Their phototransduction system and the convergence of rod signals onto downstream retinal circuits allow weak illumination to influence the visual pathway. This sensitivity is valuable when few photons are available.

The trade-off is that rod-based vision provides poorer spatial detail and does not supply the cone system's color discrimination. Cones perform better under brighter conditions and are central to high-acuity color vision. Ganglion and bipolar cells are important retinal neurons, but they are not the rod-or-cone photoreceptor class requested here. Do not confuse the cell that detects light with the cell that sends propagated spikes down the optic nerve. Retinal ganglion cells provide much of that output after the photoreceptor and intermediate circuits have processed the signal.

## w06-mc06

High visual acuity requires neighboring points in an image to remain distinguishable in the neural output. The fovea contains densely packed cones and pathways with relatively low convergence, so input from nearby cones is less likely to be pooled into one indistinguishable signal. **A** correctly links this organization with fine spatial resolution.

Extensive convergence can improve sensitivity by combining weak signals, but it sacrifices information about exactly which receptor was stimulated. That is useful for dim-light detection, not optimal for discriminating small details in bright light. The fovea's high acuity is therefore not explained by rods alone, absence of ganglion cells, or absence of receptors. Optical focusing, receptor density, and retinal circuitry all contribute. The core principle tested here is that **preserving separate channels preserves spatial detail**, whereas pooling many channels favors sensitivity.

## w06-mc07

Light activates the rod's rhodopsin pathway: activated rhodopsin stimulates transducin, which activates phosphodiesterase. Phosphodiesterase lowers cGMP, so cGMP-gated cation channels in the outer segment close. Inward current decreases while outward potassium current continues, shifting the membrane to a more negative potential. Thus **B**, closure with hyperpolarization, is correct.

This is a common point of confusion because many sensory examples begin with stimulus-induced depolarization. Rods use a different starting state: in darkness they already carry a depolarizing dark current and release glutamate tonically. Light reduces that current and decreases glutamate release. It does not convert the channels into nicotinic receptors or directly release skeletal-muscle SR calcium. Follow the sign at each step: more light leads to less cGMP, less inward current, a more negative voltage, and less transmitter release.

## w06-mc08

In darkness, cGMP levels keep cyclic-nucleotide-gated cation channels in the rod outer segment open. Their inward current counterbalances part of the outward potassium current, leaving the rod relatively depolarized compared with its illuminated state. This is the **dark current**, and it makes **A** correct.

Relatively depolarized does not mean that the rod must be producing a continuous train of action potentials. Rods communicate primarily through graded voltage changes and corresponding changes in glutamate release. When light activates rhodopsin, cGMP falls, the inward channels close, and transmitter release decreases. The rod is therefore not electrically silent in darkness and merely switched on by light. Permanently closed potassium channels and nicotinic ACh signaling do not describe the mechanism. Compare the balance of inward and outward currents in the two states to understand why illumination hyperpolarizes the cell.

## w06-mc09

Axons from ganglion cells in the **nasal half of each retina cross at the optic chiasm**, whereas temporal retinal axons remain on the same side. This makes **B** correct. The partial crossing allows visual information from the same half of visual space, received by the two eyes, to be routed toward the same cerebral hemisphere.

Do not equate a retinal half with the same-named half of the visual field. The optics of the eye reverse the image, so a stimulus in the left visual field projects to the right halves of the two retinas. The combination of crossing nasal fibers and uncrossed temporal fibers then sends that field's information to the right hemisphere. The fibers neither become motor neurons nor project principally to the spinal cord. The chiasm reorganizes visual information by **visual hemifield**, not simply by left eye versus right eye.

## w06-mc10

The skeletal-muscle motor end plate contains **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors**, which are ligand-gated cation channels. ACh binding opens them; at the resting muscle potential, the combined sodium and potassium currents produce a net inward current and a depolarizing end-plate potential. This is why **B** is correct.

The end-plate potential is not yet the propagated muscle action potential. It brings nearby voltage-gated sodium channels to threshold, initiating an action potential that spreads along the sarcolemma and T-tubules. Muscarinic M2 receptors mediate important parasympathetic effects in the heart but are not the principal motor-end-plate receptors. Alpha1 receptors respond to adrenergic signals, and rhodopsin initiates rod phototransduction. Identify both the transmitter and the receptor type: ACh can act at different receptors in different tissues, and those receptor types determine the response.

## w06-mc11

The arriving motor-neuron action potential depolarizes the axon terminal and opens **voltage-gated calcium channels**. Calcium enters the terminal and activates the vesicle-fusion machinery, allowing ACh to be released by exocytosis. Therefore **A** identifies the immediate link between the electrical signal and transmitter release.

This calcium is entering the **presynaptic nerve terminal**. Later in the pathway, a different calcium event occurs inside the muscle fiber when the SR releases calcium for contraction. Keeping the compartments separate prevents a common error: nerve-terminal calcium triggers secretion, whereas muscle cytosolic calcium permits cross-bridge activation. Ligand-gated chloride channels are not the normal release trigger, funny channels are associated with pacemaker physiology, and the neuromuscular junction is a chemical synapse rather than a gap-junction connection. Trace terminal depolarization to calcium entry before discussing ACh binding on the muscle.

## w06-mc12

A **sarcomere** is one repeating contractile unit extending from one Z disc to the next, so **A** is correct. Thin filaments are anchored at the Z discs and extend inward toward thick filaments. Many sarcomeres arranged end to end form a myofibril, allowing small shortening in each unit to contribute to substantial fiber shortening.

The boundaries matter when interpreting a contraction diagram. As thin filaments slide farther alongside thick filaments, the Z discs move closer together and the sarcomere shortens. Neither the motor neuron nor the tendon marks the boundaries of an individual sarcomere. The SR and sarcolemma are membrane systems associated with excitation and calcium handling, not the two ends of this repeating filament unit. Distinguish the scale of the whole muscle, the muscle cell, the myofibril, and the sarcomere before assigning anatomical labels.

## w06-mc13

The **sarcoplasmic reticulum**, or SR, is an intracellular membrane network specialized for calcium storage, release, and reuptake. During activation, SR calcium release raises cytosolic calcium so that it can bind troponin and permit cross-bridge cycling. During relaxation, calcium is pumped back into the SR. This makes **A** correct.

The SR is not the same structure as a T-tubule. T-tubules are invaginations of the cell surface membrane that carry the action potential into the fiber; they communicate the electrical command to the adjacent SR. ACh is released from the motor-neuron terminal rather than synthesized as the SR's main function. Oxygen transport by hemoglobin and sound detection by hair cells belong to different systems. The SR's role is to make intracellular calcium availability change rapidly and reversibly in response to muscle excitation.

## w06-mc14

A skeletal muscle fiber is large enough that excitation confined to its outer surface would not efficiently coordinate all its internal contractile machinery. **T-tubules** are inward extensions of the sarcolemma that bring the membrane depolarization close to SR release sites throughout the fiber. **A** therefore states their principal role.

The action potential travels along T-tubule membrane, where voltage sensors communicate with the SR's ryanodine receptors. Calcium is then released near the myofibrils, helping contraction occur rapidly across the cell. T-tubules do not form a myelin sheath and are not principally an oxygen store or ATP-producing organelle. Their lumen remains continuous with the extracellular space, whereas the SR contains an intracellular calcium store. Separating **electrical delivery by T-tubules** from **calcium release by SR** clarifies why both membrane systems are needed for excitation–contraction coupling.

## w06-mc15

Calcium initiates skeletal-muscle thin-filament activation by binding the calcium-binding component of **troponin**. This changes the position of the troponin–tropomyosin complex so that myosin can interact effectively with binding sites on actin. **B** is correct.

Calcium is therefore a regulatory signal, not the fuel that powers the myosin motor. ATP supports the cross-bridge cycle and calcium pumping, while calcium controls whether the thin filament permits productive binding. Myosin has ATPase activity, but direct calcium binding to myosin ATPase is not the principal skeletal-muscle switch asked about here. Titin contributes elasticity and thick-filament positioning, and nebulin helps organize thin filaments. A complete mechanism links increased cytosolic calcium to troponin, tropomyosin movement, exposed actin sites, and cross-bridge formation rather than stopping at the statement that calcium causes contraction.

## w06-mc16

The question specifies **ATP binding**, which causes myosin to detach from actin. Thus **A** is correct. ATP hydrolysis is a subsequent event that returns the myosin head to an energized, cocked configuration so another cycle can occur when an appropriate actin site is available.

These steps should not be compressed into the vague claim that ATP simply makes myosin pull. The power stroke is associated with transitions after actin binding and phosphate release; a new ATP molecule is needed to break the strong actin–myosin attachment. Without sufficient ATP, cross-bridges cannot detach normally, contributing to rigor. Calcium exposes binding sites by acting through troponin and tropomyosin; it does not replace ATP at myosin. Nicotinic-channel opening and the motor-neuron action potential occur upstream. Distinguish ATP binding, ATP hydrolysis, and calcium regulation as separate parts of the mechanism.

## w06-mc17

During contraction, myosin heads generate movement of thin filaments relative to thick filaments. The filaments increasingly overlap and the Z discs move closer together, so the sarcomere shortens. The filaments themselves do not substantially shorten. **B** is the central statement of the **sliding-filament model**.

This predicts specific changes in a sarcomere diagram: the A band, which corresponds to thick-filament length, remains essentially constant, while the I band and H zone become smaller during shortening. Z discs move toward one another, not farther apart. Sarcomeres remain recognizable repeating units rather than disappearing. The distinction between filament length and sarcomere length is the main conceptual test. Also remember that muscle can generate force without shortening if external conditions prevent movement; cross-bridge activity and whole-muscle shortening are related but not identical outcomes.

## w06-mc18

In skeletal muscle, depolarization of the T-tubule membrane activates a voltage-sensitive protein often called the **dihydropyridine receptor**. Its conformational change is mechanically coupled to ryanodine receptors in the adjacent SR, causing calcium release into the cytosol. **A** identifies this critical connection.

The relationship explains how an electrical event at the cell membrane rapidly controls a calcium store inside the cell. Once calcium binds troponin, thin-filament inhibition is relieved and cross-bridge cycling can begin. Do not substitute a baroreceptor or an olfactory receptor: those detect pressure-related stretch or odorants in different systems. Also distinguish skeletal from cardiac coupling. In ventricular myocytes, calcium entering through L-type channels triggers further SR release; in skeletal muscle, the direct voltage-sensor coupling is the central mechanism. Similar channel names do not make the two activation schemes identical.

## w06-mc19

Relaxation requires cytosolic calcium to fall so that calcium leaves troponin and tropomyosin can again inhibit actin–myosin interaction. **SERCA**, an ATP-dependent calcium pump in the SR membrane, moves calcium back into the SR and therefore makes **B** correct.

Simply stopping motor-neuron firing is not enough to describe the intracellular mechanism. The calcium already released must be removed from the cytosol. Persistently high calcium would continue to permit cross-bridge cycling, while continued ACh release would promote additional excitation. Relaxation does not require irreversible actin destruction; the contractile apparatus is reused. ATP is needed for calcium reuptake and for myosin detachment, so relaxation is not equivalent to all energy use stopping. Follow the chain from removal of the activating signal to restoration of low cytosolic calcium and renewed inhibition of the thin filament.

## w06-mc20

A single skeletal-muscle action potential triggers a brief calcium transient and a corresponding mechanical response called a **twitch**. This makes **A** correct. The twitch includes a short delay before measurable force, a contraction phase, and relaxation as cytosolic calcium falls and cross-bridge activity decreases.

The electrical action potential is much shorter than the full mechanical twitch. This difference in timing is important because another action potential can arrive while force from the first response persists, allowing temporal summation. Tetanus requires sufficiently frequent repeated stimulation; it is not the necessary consequence of one action potential. A cardiac cycle is an integrated sequence of heart events, and an ECG P wave reflects atrial depolarization. Keep the scale of the question in view: it concerns one skeletal-muscle excitation and its resulting brief force response, not whole-heart electrical or pumping activity.

## w06-mc21

Temporal summation occurs when a second activation arrives before force and calcium-dependent activation from the first twitch have fully returned to baseline. The second response builds on the remaining activation, increasing total tension. **A** is therefore correct.

The key is that the **mechanical responses** can overlap even though the individual muscle action potentials remain separate, all-or-none events. With higher stimulation frequencies, repeated calcium release can maintain substantial thin-filament activation and produce increasingly sustained force. This is different from recruitment, which adds more motor units rather than increasing activation frequency within already active units. Silent units cannot produce summation, and an infinitely stretched sarcomere would not provide normal overlap for force generation. An optic-nerve lesion is unrelated. To explain the answer, compare the short electrical event with the longer calcium and force responses it initiates.

## w06-mc22

A **motor unit** consists of one alpha motor neuron and all the skeletal muscle fibers that it innervates. When that neuron fires under normal conditions, its axon branches convey the signal to its associated fibers. This makes **A** correct.

Motor units vary in size. Small units provide fine increments of force and are useful for precise control, while larger units can add more force when recruited. The unit is therefore a functional organization of neural control, not merely one anatomical segment of a muscle. A sarcomere and tendon do not form a motor unit, and a sensory neuron with its receptor is an afferent arrangement rather than a motor one. A heart chamber pair is at a different biological scale. Distinguish a **muscle fiber**, which is one cell, from a **motor unit**, which usually includes multiple fibers under one motor neuron's control.

## w06-mc23

As force demand increases, the nervous system generally recruits **smaller, lower-threshold motor neurons and their motor units before larger ones**. Smaller units are commonly associated with more fatigue-resistant fibers and provide fine increments of force. Additional, larger units are recruited as stronger output is needed. This makes **B** correct.

The size principle concerns the order in which units join the active population. It does not mean that the active units' individual action potentials become larger, nor that every motor unit must turn on at once. Simultaneous recruitment of all units would make fine control difficult and needlessly involve high-force units during small tasks. Sensory neurons inform motor control but are not themselves motor units. Recruitment can operate alongside increased firing frequency: one mechanism adds more fibers, while the other changes how frequently already active fibers are stimulated.

## w06-mc24

At long sarcomere lengths, thick and thin filaments overlap less. Fewer myosin heads can reach actin binding sites, so fewer force-producing cross-bridges can form. Active force therefore falls when a sarcomere is stretched far beyond its optimal range, making **A** correct.

This is a geometrical limitation of the contractile apparatus, not necessarily a failure of motor-neuron signaling, ATP binding, or SR identity. ATP and calcium can be available while filament overlap is still inadequate. It is also important to distinguish **active tension** from **passive tension**. Elastic structures can resist stretch and contribute passive force even when cross-bridge-generated active force declines. The question asks specifically about active force. When reading a length–tension graph, check which component is plotted before concluding that every kind of measured force must decrease at long lengths.

## w06-mc25

The correct hierarchy is **whole muscle → fascicle → muscle fiber → myofibril → sarcomere**, so **A** is correct. A whole muscle contains bundles called fascicles. Each fascicle contains muscle fibers, which are individual elongated cells. Each fiber contains many myofibrils, and each myofibril consists of repeated sarcomeres arranged end to end.

This nested organization explains how molecular sliding can become macroscopic force and movement. Sarcomeres within a myofibril contribute shortening in series, while many contractile elements across the fiber and muscle contribute to overall force. The alternatives mix levels or reverse the containment relationships. Avoid using fiber and myofibril interchangeably: the fiber is the whole cell, with membranes, nuclei, and organelles, whereas myofibrils are its internal contractile assemblies. Asking "what contains what?" is the simplest way to reconstruct the order without memorizing an arbitrary list.

## w06-mc26

**Myosin** is the motor protein whose heads bind actin, hydrolyze ATP, and undergo the conformational changes that generate force. It is therefore the correct answer, **B**. Thick filaments contain myosin molecules positioned so their heads can interact repeatedly with the surrounding thin filaments.

Actin supplies the track and binding sites but is not the ATPase motor making the power stroke. Tropomyosin helps regulate access to those sites, while titin contributes elasticity and positioning. Each protein is important, but their functions are not interchangeable. To explain muscle contraction, distinguish the motor, the track, the regulatory system, and the elastic support. The power stroke also depends on the correct cross-bridge sequence: calcium permits productive binding, myosin transitions generate force, ATP binding allows detachment, and ATP hydrolysis prepares the head for another cycle.

When many photoreceptors converge onto fewer downstream neurons, their signals can be pooled. However, the output then identifies the contributing receptor less precisely, reducing **spatial acuity**. This trade-off makes **A** correct.

Imagine ten receptors sharing one output channel. Activity in that channel indicates that light affected the group, but not necessarily which member detected it. ## w06-mc28

A competitive blocker at the nicotinic ACh receptor reduces ACh's ability to activate the postsynaptic cation channels. Fewer muscle fibers then generate effective action potentials, so calcium release and force decline. This makes **A** the direct physiological consequence.

## w06-sr02

Rods and cones solve different visual problems. **Rods** are highly sensitive and support vision under dim illumination. Their signals often converge extensively, which helps weak input influence downstream circuits but reduces spatial precision. Rod-based vision does not provide the cone system's color discrimination, and rods are numerous outside the central fovea.

## w06-sr03

Start with the dark baseline. In darkness, cGMP keeps outer-segment cation channels open, generating a depolarizing dark current. The rod releases glutamate tonically. When a photon activates rhodopsin, the associated retinal chromophore changes configuration, and activated opsin stimulates **transducin**. Transducin activates phosphodiesterase, which lowers cGMP.

With less cGMP available, cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels close. Inward current falls while potassium continues to leave the cell, so the membrane hyperpolarizes. This more negative voltage reduces calcium entry at the synaptic terminal and decreases glutamate release. The central message is therefore **light → less cGMP → fewer open cation channels → hyperpolarization → less glutamate**.

Do not describe the rod as generating a larger action potential when light increases. Its primary signal is a graded change in voltage and transmitter release; downstream retinal circuits transform that change, and ganglion cells provide propagated optic-nerve spikes. Also avoid saying less glutamate simply shuts off vision: ON and OFF pathways respond differently to that change. The question asks how the rod's output changes, not for one universal response of every downstream cell.

## w06-sr04

Begin with retinal ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerves. At the optic chiasm, axons from the **nasal halves of the retinas cross**, while temporal retinal axons remain ipsilateral. Beyond the chiasm they travel in the optic tracts. The major conscious visual pathway then relays through the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and projects through the optic radiations to visual cortex.

The partial crossing organizes information by visual field. Because the optics reverse the image, the left visual field stimulates the right halves of the two retinas. The crossing arrangement brings that information together in the right hemisphere; the right visual field is represented principally in the left hemisphere. This is not equivalent to sending every signal from the left eye to the right brain.

For a diagram, label the eyes, nasal and temporal retina, optic nerves, chiasm, tracts, thalamic relay, and cortex. Then trace one visual hemifield through both eyes using a consistent line style. ## w06-sr05

An action potential arriving at the motor-neuron terminal opens presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium enters the terminal and triggers ACh-containing vesicles to fuse with the membrane. Released ACh diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds nicotinic receptors on the motor end plate.

These receptors are ligand-gated cation channels. At the resting muscle potential, their opening produces net inward current and a graded **end-plate potential**. If this depolarization reaches threshold in adjacent excitable membrane, voltage-gated sodium channels generate a muscle-fiber action potential. The action potential then propagates along the sarcolemma and into T-tubules, linking transmission to excitation–contraction coupling.

Two distinctions are essential. First, calcium in the nerve terminal triggers transmitter release; calcium later released from the muscle SR activates contraction. Second, the end-plate potential is a local graded response, whereas the muscle action potential is propagated and all-or-none. ACh is subsequently broken down by acetylcholinesterase, helping terminate receptor activation. An answer that jumps directly from "nerve fires" to "muscle contracts" omits the chemical synapse and the threshold step that the question is testing.

## w06-sr06

A sarcomere runs from one Z disc to the next. Thin filaments containing **actin** anchor at the Z discs and project inward; thick filaments containing **myosin** occupy the central region. Myosin heads interact with actin to form force-generating cross-bridges. The arrangement allows thin filaments to slide toward the center while the Z discs move closer together.

The other proteins give this motor system control and structure. **Tropomyosin** lies along actin and helps block myosin-binding sites at low calcium. **Troponin** binds calcium and changes the position of the regulatory complex, permitting productive actin–myosin interaction. **Titin** contributes elastic support and helps position thick filaments within the sarcomere. **Nebulin** associates with thin filaments and supports their organization and length regulation.

A complete answer assigns a distinct role to each protein rather than calling all of them contractile. Actin is the track, myosin the ATP-dependent motor, troponin and tropomyosin the regulatory apparatus, and titin and nebulin important structural components. When shortening occurs, filament overlap changes; the individual thick and thin filaments do not simply shrink. Use the Z discs and central thick filament as anchors for a labeled sketch.

## w06-sr07

Calcium first binds troponin, shifting tropomyosin so that actin sites become available. An energized myosin head carrying ADP and phosphate can then bind actin. Transitions associated with phosphate release strengthen the interaction and initiate the **power stroke**, moving the thin filament relative to the thick filament. ADP is subsequently released.

The strongly attached myosin head must detach before repeating the movement. A new ATP molecule binds myosin and lowers its affinity for actin, allowing detachment. ATP hydrolysis then returns the head to an energized position, with ADP and phosphate retained. If calcium remains high enough to keep sites available, another cross-bridge cycle can follow.

Calcium and ATP thus have different jobs. Calcium regulates access to the actin sites; ATP is required for detachment and preparation of the motor for repeated cycling. ATP also supports calcium reuptake during relaxation. Lack of calcium prevents normal thin-filament activation, whereas lack of ATP prevents normal detachment and can lead to rigor. A strong narrative specifies these distinct stages rather than treating ATP binding as the power stroke itself.

## w06-sr08

The muscle action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and down T-tubules, bringing depolarization close to SR calcium-release sites. T-tubule voltage sensors, commonly termed dihydropyridine receptors, change conformation and activate nearby **ryanodine receptors** in the SR. Calcium stored in the SR is released into the cytosol.

The rise in cytosolic calcium allows binding to troponin. Troponin–tropomyosin changes position, reducing inhibition of actin–myosin interaction. Myosin heads can now form productive cross-bridges and cycle using ATP, generating force and, when the load permits, shortening. Excitation–contraction coupling is therefore the chain connecting a membrane electrical event to mechanical activity in the myofibrils.

Keep this skeletal-muscle mechanism distinct from cardiac coupling. In skeletal muscle, direct coupling between the T-tubule voltage sensor and SR release channel is central. In ventricular muscle, calcium entry through surface L-type channels acts as the trigger for further SR release. Also distinguish force generation from obligatory shortening: an activated muscle can develop tension against a fixed load without changing overall length. Include the electrical signal, calcium-release step, thin-filament regulation, and ATP-dependent motor response.

## w06-sr09

When motor-neuron firing stops, no new action potentials arrive to trigger additional ACh release. ACh already in the cleft is rapidly broken down by acetylcholinesterase, so nicotinic receptors are no longer persistently activated. Without further effective end-plate potentials, new muscle action potentials cease.

Inside the fiber, calcium must be removed from the cytosol. **SERCA pumps** use ATP to return calcium to the SR. As free cytosolic calcium decreases, calcium dissociates from troponin and tropomyosin again inhibits myosin access to actin sites. Productive cross-bridge cycling declines and active force falls. ATP is also required for myosin to detach from actin.

This explains why relaxation is not simply an absence of all biochemical activity. It requires active calcium transport and normal cross-bridge detachment. Stopping the nerve signal while leaving cytosolic calcium high would not fully explain relaxation, and removing ATP would not produce ordinary relaxed muscle. A complete answer connects the termination of synaptic drive to the intracellular restoration of the low-calcium resting state, rather than treating these as unrelated events.

## w06-sr10

**Temporal summation** changes stimulation frequency within already active muscle fibers. A new action potential arrives before the earlier twitch has completely relaxed, producing another calcium release while some calcium-dependent activation and force persist. The mechanical responses add. At sufficiently high frequencies, contractions become increasingly fused; sustained high force is called tetanus. This does not mean that action potentials themselves merge into one giant spike.

**Motor-unit recruitment** increases force by activating additional motor neurons and their muscle fibers. More parallel contractile elements contribute to the muscle's total output. According to the size principle, smaller, lower-threshold, generally fatigue-resistant units are recruited before larger units as force demand rises.

These mechanisms can operate together but answer different questions: frequency modulation asks how often active units are stimulated, while recruitment asks how many units are active. A complete answer must explain both rather than using the terms interchangeably. Skeletal muscle can summate because its electrical refractory period is short relative to its mechanical twitch. That timing contrasts with cardiac muscle, whose prolonged refractory period normally prevents the kind of repeated activation needed for tetanus.

## w06-sr11

Active tension depends on how many productive cross-bridges can form at a given sarcomere length. Near the optimal length, thick and thin filaments overlap sufficiently for many myosin heads to interact with actin without excessive structural interference. Active force is therefore high.

When the sarcomere is stretched too far, overlap decreases and fewer myosin heads can reach actin. At the extreme, no overlap means no active cross-bridge force. At very short lengths, excessive thin-filament overlap and mechanical interference reduce effective force production. Thus active tension is not a simple rule that either more stretch or more shortening always produces more force.

Separate this active relationship from **passive tension**, which arises when elastic structures resist stretch. Passive tension can increase at long lengths even while active cross-bridge force declines. A graph of total tension combines both components. In an explanation or sketch, identify the horizontal axis as length, state which tension component is being plotted, and relate each part of the active curve to filament geometry. ATP and calcium are necessary for activation, but their presence cannot compensate for an absence of appropriate overlap.

## w06-sr12

Localize the toxin's effect before predicting the outcome. The motor neuron can still generate an action potential, release ACh, and activate nicotinic receptors because those steps are stated to be intact. End-plate potentials can therefore reach threshold and initiate muscle action potentials. The failure occurs after membrane excitation, at the step that should release calcium from the SR.

Without a sufficient rise in cytosolic calcium, troponin is not adequately activated. Tropomyosin continues to inhibit productive actin–myosin interaction, so cross-bridge force is greatly reduced or absent despite electrical activity in the muscle membrane. The exact reduction depends on how completely calcium release is blocked.

This is an example of **electrical excitation being uncoupled from mechanical contraction**. It differs from nicotinic-receptor blockade, which prevents a normal end-plate response earlier in the pathway, and from ATP depletion, which affects cross-bridge detachment and calcium pumping. A strong answer preserves the upstream functions that the question explicitly says are normal and follows the downstream consequences of the specific lesion. Do not conclude that the muscle action potential must disappear merely because contraction fails.
