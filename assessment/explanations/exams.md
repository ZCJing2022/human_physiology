# Exam-specific explanation narratives

The three existing forms share some prompts. Identical prompts receive the same full explanation; no explanation is shortened because a question appears in an exam. Multiple-choice explanations are matched to the corresponding weekly question by the complete stem and options.

## exam-sr01

The response illustrates **sensory adaptation**: firing changes even though the pressure stimulus continues. The initial deformation produces a strong receptor response and high afferent firing. During a sustained stimulus, the receptor apparatus and associated signaling adapt, so activity declines. A rapid decline is characteristic of a phasic response; a gradual decline with substantial persistent firing is more tonic. A decline alone does not identify every detail of the receptor type.

The CNS can still obtain duration information from the pattern that remains and from other receptor populations. Slowly adapting receptors continue to signal the ongoing pressure, while rapidly adapting receptors emphasize the beginning, changes, and sometimes the end of stimulation. Combining those channels allows the nervous system to represent both events and continuing states.

The key mistake is to infer that a falling firing rate proves the physical stimulus is becoming weaker or has ended. Here the question explicitly holds the pressure stimulus sustained. Likewise, adaptation does not mean that each action potential gradually shrinks. A complete answer identifies the changing firing pattern, explains the tonic–phasic distinction, and describes how parallel sensory channels preserve useful duration information. It should distinguish what the stated observation establishes from what would require further measurements.

## exam-sr02

Explain the illuminated response by first establishing the dark state. In darkness, cGMP keeps outer-segment cation channels open, producing an inward dark current. Rods are relatively depolarized and release glutamate tonically. Light activates rhodopsin, which stimulates transducin and then phosphodiesterase. Phosphodiesterase lowers cGMP, causing cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels to close.

The fall in inward current leaves outward potassium current less opposed, so the membrane becomes more negative: the rod **hyperpolarizes**. At the synaptic terminal, the more negative voltage reduces calcium entry and therefore decreases glutamate release. The answer must connect both parts of the question—why voltage changes and why transmitter output changes—rather than ending with the phrase "light activates rhodopsin."

This is not a typical depolarizing sensory response and not a larger rod action potential. The rod communicates through graded voltage and transmitter changes. A fall in glutamate also does not mean that the entire visual pathway becomes inactive: downstream ON and OFF circuits interpret that change differently. For a timed answer, a clearly explained chain from rhodopsin to cGMP, channel closure, hyperpolarization, calcium entry, and reduced glutamate contains the essential causal links.

## exam-sr03

The toxin is specified to block **postsynaptic nicotinic ACh receptors**, so begin by preserving the upstream functions that remain normal. The motor-neuron action potential can arrive at the terminal, open calcium channels, and trigger ACh release. The released transmitter, however, cannot activate the blocked receptor population effectively. The end-plate potential is reduced and may fail to reach the threshold needed to generate a muscle action potential.

If the muscle action potential fails, T-tubule voltage sensors are not activated normally, SR calcium release is insufficient, troponin is not adequately activated, and tropomyosin continues to inhibit productive cross-bridge formation. Muscle force therefore falls. With partial blockade, some fibers may still reach threshold, so weakness rather than complete absence of contraction is possible.

The causal chain is **receptor blockade → reduced end-plate depolarization → failed or reduced muscle excitation → reduced calcium activation → reduced force**. Do not say that the toxin necessarily stops the motor-neuron action potential or destroys the contractile proteins; those are not the stated lesion. Also distinguish an end-plate potential from a propagated action potential. The question tests whether you can locate a failure at one stage and predict its downstream consequences without inventing additional damage.

## exam-sr04

The two mechanisms increase force at different organizational levels. Increasing **stimulation frequency** activates the same muscle fibers more often. Because a twitch and its calcium-dependent activation last longer than the brief muscle action potential, another action potential can arrive before complete relaxation. The new calcium release adds to residual activation, allowing temporal summation and, at sufficiently high frequencies, increasingly fused force or tetanus.

Increasing **motor-unit recruitment** adds more active motor neurons and the muscle fibers they innervate. More contractile elements then contribute in parallel to total muscle force. This does not require each previously active fiber to change its stimulation frequency. The size principle generally brings smaller, lower-threshold units into activity before larger units as demand rises.

A complete comparison should explicitly say **more frequent activation of active fibers versus activation of more fibers**. Do not claim that individual action potentials become larger, that recruitment means more cross-bridges inside one unchanged fiber only, or that the two processes cannot occur together. In normal force control they often cooperate. Explain why the electrical and mechanical time scales allow frequency summation, then explain how neural organization permits graded recruitment of additional units.

## exam-sr05

Excitation starts in the SA node and spreads through atrial myocardium. It reaches the AV node, where conduction slows, then passes into the AV bundle, right and left bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers before spreading through ventricular myocardium. This sequence coordinates atrial activation with the later, organized contraction of the ventricles.

The **AV nodal delay** is central to the functional explanation. It allows atrial contraction to contribute to the final portion of ventricular filling before ventricular pressure rises and closes the AV valves. Most filling has already occurred passively, so the delay should not be described as allowing the atria to perform all filling. Rapid conduction through the downstream His–Purkinje system then helps distribute excitation for effective ejection.

The question asks for both a route and a reason. Listing SA, AV, His, and Purkinje without explaining timing is incomplete, as is saying that the AV node simply "slows the heart" without identifying the atrial–ventricular coordination. Keep blood flow and electrical flow separate: the impulse does not pass through valves in the way a red cell does. Also distinguish the SA node's automatic initiating rate from conduction velocity in the various tissues.

## exam-sr06

The **QRS complex records ventricular depolarization**, not ejection itself. Depolarization initiates excitation–contraction coupling and force development, so ventricular pressure begins to rise after electrical activation. When ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure, the AV valves close. This event is associated with S1 and marks the beginning of the closed-valve isovolumetric contraction phase.

During that interval, ventricular pressure continues rising while volume remains essentially at EDV because both the inlet and outlet valves are closed. Only when ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the corresponding artery do the semilunar valves open. For the left heart, the comparison is left-ventricular versus aortic pressure. Opening then permits ejection, and ventricular volume begins to fall.

A complete answer must preserve these intermediate steps. Saying "QRS opens the aortic valve" skips pressure generation and the isovolumetric phase. Saying the AV valves open during the pressure rise reverses the normal sequence. On a Wiggers diagram, locate QRS first, then follow the ventricular-pressure trace through the atrial and aortic pressure crossings. This is how an electrical landmark is connected causally—not merely by memorized coincidence—to valve events and blood flow.

## exam-sr07

Begin by translating the volumes into their physiological meanings. The ventricle contains **125 mL at end diastole** and retains **55 mL at end systole**. The ejected amount is the difference: SV = EDV − ESV = 125 − 55 = **70 mL/beat**. The remaining 55 mL is not the stroke volume.

Next use the given heart rate to convert a per-beat volume into a per-minute flow. CO = HR × SV = 80 beats/min × 70 mL/beat = **5,600 mL/min = 5.6 L/min**. The beat units cancel, and division by 1,000 converts milliliters to liters. Reporting 5.6 mL/min would lose the conversion scale, while adding HR to SV would combine incompatible units.

Check that SV is positive and less than EDV and that the resulting CO is expressed as volume per time. These arithmetic checks reinforce the underlying distinction between the filled volume, the retained volume, and the amount pumped each minute. The output refers to one ventricle's flow; do not double it by adding the left and right ventricular outputs, which normally carry approximately equal average flow through circuits in series.

## exam-sr08

Increased venous return supplies more blood for ventricular filling. With sufficient filling time, **EDV increases**, increasing end-diastolic myocardial stretch. Within the physiological operating range, length-dependent activation raises the force of the next contraction and tends to increase the amount ejected. This is the intrinsic **Frank–Starling mechanism**.

The resulting increase in stroke volume helps the heart match output to the blood returning to it. If heart rate is initially unchanged, CO = HR × SV predicts that cardiac output increases as well. The question is asking for this immediate filling-dependent response, not necessarily a simultaneous sympathetic rise in contractility. Positive inotropy would increase performance at a given preload through a different initiating change, such as altered calcium handling.

State the assumptions explicitly: filling can actually increase, the heart is operating within an appropriate length range, and other loading conditions are comparable. More stretch is not beneficial without limit, and changes in afterload or very short filling time could modify the result. A strong response links venous return to EDV, EDV to length-dependent force and SV, and SV to CO, rather than simply asserting that the heart "works harder."
