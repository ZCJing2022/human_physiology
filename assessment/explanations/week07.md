# Week 7 explanation narratives

## w07-mc01

The lecture contrasts two functional populations of **excitable cardiac muscle cells**: contractile myocytes and autorhythmic cells. Contractile myocytes form the force-producing bulk of the myocardium, while a smaller specialized population initiates and conducts excitation. Within that comparison, **B** is correct.

Contractile cells contain organized actin and myosin and generate the tension needed to eject blood. Pacemaker cells are crucial because they organize the rhythm, but their importance does not make them the predominant force-producing population. Sensory neurons and erythrocytes are not alternative cardiac muscle-cell types; erythrocytes pass through the circulation rather than forming the heart's contractile wall. The wording should not be interpreted as a census of every cell type in heart tissue, which also includes endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and other nonmuscle cells. The distinction being tested is **rhythm initiation versus mechanical pumping**.

## w07-mc02

Gap junctions form channels connecting the cytoplasm of adjacent cardiac cells. Ions can move through these low-resistance connections, allowing depolarizing current in one myocyte to help bring a neighboring cell to threshold. **A** is therefore correct: gap junctions provide electrical coupling.

This enables excitation to spread through myocardium rather than requiring a separate motor-neuron command to every cell, as in skeletal muscle. Intercalated discs also contain mechanical junctions that help cells withstand the forces of contraction; electrical and mechanical coupling are complementary but distinct roles. Gap junctions do not store calcium like the SR, prevent all ionic movement, or synthesize hemoglobin. A useful way to distinguish structures is to ask whether they transmit current, hold cells together, or manage intracellular calcium. Gap junctions specifically support coordinated electrical activation across neighboring myocytes.

## w07-mc03

During a ventricular action potential, L-type calcium channels open in the surface and T-tubule membrane. The entering calcium activates nearby SR ryanodine receptors, causing a larger release of stored calcium into the cytosol. This process is **calcium-induced calcium release**, making **A** correct.

The initial calcium entry is a trigger, not the only calcium participating in contraction. The combined rise in cytosolic calcium activates troponin and allows cross-bridge cycling. This distinguishes ventricular coupling from the direct voltage-sensor-to-SR coupling emphasized for skeletal muscle. Nicotinic ACh receptors belong to neuromuscular transmission, and calcium does not become the iron in hemoglobin. Nor does normal calcium entry permanently close all potassium channels. Follow the sequence from membrane excitation to trigger calcium, amplified SR release, and thin-filament activation; confusing those stages obscures why extracellular calcium is important to cardiac contraction.

## w07-mc04

For cardiac muscle to relax, calcium must leave the cytosol so that troponin activation and cross-bridge cycling decline. **SERCA** returns calcium to the SR, while membrane transport mechanisms, importantly sodium–calcium exchange, remove calcium from the cell. Thus **A** names processes that directly lower cytosolic calcium.

SERCA uses ATP directly. Sodium–calcium exchange usually uses the inward sodium electrochemical gradient to support calcium extrusion; that sodium gradient is maintained by the sodium–potassium pump. These mechanisms therefore differ in how they are energized, even though both contribute to calcium removal. Opening nicotinic receptors, activating rhodopsin, or aggregating platelets does not provide the normal myocyte relaxation mechanism. Relaxation is an active restoration of the low-calcium state, not merely the absence of electrical stimulation. The heart needs that calcium decline to release force and permit filling before the next beat.

## w07-mc05

The right ventricle pumps blood into the **pulmonary artery**, which carries it to lung capillaries. After pulmonary gas exchange, blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium and then enters the left ventricle through the mitral valve. **B** gives this route in the correct order.

The names artery and vein describe direction relative to the heart, not oxygen content. Pulmonary arteries carry blood away from the heart even though that blood is relatively oxygen-poor; pulmonary veins return relatively oxygen-rich blood. The aorta begins the systemic circuit after left-ventricular ejection, while the venae cavae return systemic blood to the right atrium. Mixing these circuits produces the incorrect alternatives. Trace one continuous blood cell journey and include the chambers on either side of the lungs rather than trying to infer vessel names from oxygenation alone.

## w07-mc06

Heart valves respond primarily to **pressure differences across them**. When pressure upstream exceeds pressure downstream in the forward direction, a valve can open. When the gradient reverses, the valve closes and limits backflow. **B** is therefore correct.

This means valve movement follows the changing pressures generated by contraction, relaxation, and blood flow. The leaflets do not actively contract like a muscle to create the opening. Papillary muscles and chordae tendineae help prevent atrioventricular valve prolapse during ventricular contraction; they do not pull the valves open to initiate filling. Electrical activation contributes indirectly by triggering muscle contraction and pressure development, but a particular ECG wave does not directly command a valve to open. Platelet activation concerns hemostasis. When analyzing the cardiac cycle, compare pressure on the two sides of a valve before deciding its position.

## w07-mc07

After the rapid ventricular upstroke, the membrane remains depolarized during a **plateau**. Inward calcium current through L-type channels counterbalances important outward potassium currents, delaying full repolarization. This balance makes **A** correct.

A nearly flat voltage trace does not mean that ions have stopped moving. It means the net current is relatively small because inward and outward components partly offset one another. Fast sodium channels contribute to the rapid upstroke but then inactivate; persistent fast sodium entry for several seconds is not the normal explanation of the plateau. Calcium entry during this phase also helps trigger SR calcium release and contraction. The prolonged electrical event extends the refractory period, preventing the rapid repeated activation that would cause skeletal-muscle-like tetanus. Connect the ionic balance to both the waveform and the heart's need to alternate contraction with filling.

## w07-mc08

Cardiac contractile cells have a prolonged action potential and a refractory period that overlaps much of their mechanical contraction. A new effective action potential cannot normally be triggered early enough to stack contractions into sustained tetanus. This makes **B** correct.

Skeletal muscle provides the contrast: its action potential is brief relative to the twitch, so repeated stimulation can arrive while force persists and cause temporal summation. The heart's timing prevents that pattern of continuous contraction and preserves opportunities for relaxation and ventricular filling. Cardiac muscle certainly contains actin and uses calcium, and autonomic nerves modify its activity. The absence of those components cannot explain the lack of tetanus. The relevant feature is the **relationship between electrical refractoriness and mechanical duration**, not an inability of cardiac cells to generate force or respond to regulatory input.

## w07-mc09

Several cardiac tissues can exhibit intrinsic automaticity, but the **SA node normally reaches threshold most frequently**. Its faster rhythm activates the heart before slower potential pacemakers complete their own spontaneous cycles. Thus **B** explains why the SA node normally sets heart rate.

This does not mean that the SA node is the only tissue capable of automatic electrical activity. Slower pacemaker regions can become important when normal initiation or conduction fails. The relevant comparison is the interval between spontaneous threshold crossings, not simply which tissue has potassium channels. Many cardiac cells have potassium conductances, and the SA node is in the right atrium rather than the left ventricle. Distinguish intrinsic pacemaker rate from conduction speed: the tissue that initiates the rhythm fastest is not necessarily the tissue that conducts an impulse most rapidly through the heart.

## w07-mc10

The **funny current**, often written If, contributes to the gradual depolarization of pacemaker cells between action potentials. Its channels are activated at relatively negative membrane potentials and carry a mixed cation current that is net inward over the relevant diastolic range. That inward current helps the membrane move toward threshold, making **A** correct.

If is not the entire pacemaker mechanism. Changes in potassium and calcium conductances also contribute to the spontaneous depolarization and subsequent action potential. Nor should the SA nodal upstroke be confused with the fast sodium-dependent upstroke of ventricular contractile cells; calcium channels are especially important in nodal depolarization. The ventricular plateau, clot formation, and skeletal-muscle tetanus are different processes. Read the question as asking which **phase of pacemaker activity** the current helps produce: the slow approach to threshold during diastole.

## w07-mc11

Normal excitation begins in the **SA node**, spreads through the atria, reaches the **AV node**, and then travels through the AV bundle, bundle branches, and **Purkinje fibers** to the ventricular myocardium. **B** preserves this essential order.

The arrangement coordinates pumping. Atrial activation precedes ventricular activation, the AV node delays conduction, and the His–Purkinje system distributes excitation rapidly through the ventricles. Reversing the sequence would not produce the normal timing of atrial contribution to filling followed by coordinated ventricular contraction. The listed answer abbreviates atrial spread between the SA and AV nodes; it does not imply that the atrial myocardium is skipped. Also keep electrical conduction separate from blood flow: the electrical impulse follows specialized pathways, whereas blood passes through chambers and valves. A correct explanation states both the sequence and why its timing is useful.

## w07-mc12

The **AV nodal delay** provides time between atrial activation and ventricular activation. Atrial contraction can add to ventricular filling before the ventricles develop high pressure and close the AV valves. This makes **A** correct.

Most ventricular filling occurs passively earlier in diastole, so the answer should not imply that the atria provide all ventricular filling. Their contraction completes the filling process under ordinary conditions, and the conduction delay helps maintain the appropriate order. The AV node does not prevent atrial depolarization, stop the SA node, or hold the semilunar valves open. Valve position depends on pressure gradients, not directly on the delay itself. The functional benefit arises because a timed electrical sequence produces a useful mechanical sequence: atrial activity first, then coordinated ventricular contraction and ejection.

## w07-mc13

The **P wave represents atrial depolarization**, making **A** correct. Depolarization activates the atrial muscle's excitation–contraction process, so atrial contraction follows the electrical event. The ECG wave and the mechanical contraction are related in time but are not the same measurement.

The QRS complex corresponds to ventricular depolarization, and the T wave corresponds to ventricular repolarization. An ECG measures potential differences at the body surface arising from the heart's combined electrical activity; it does not directly measure atrial force. A large or small P wave should therefore not be read as a direct force scale. Also distinguish the tiny SA nodal initiating activity from the larger atrial depolarization recorded as P. When relating the ECG to a Wiggers diagram, place the electrical activation before the resulting pressure and volume consequences rather than labeling P as atrial contraction itself.

## w07-mc14

The **QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization**, so **B** is correct. Electrical activation spreads through the ventricles and initiates the calcium-dependent processes that develop contractile force. Ventricular pressure rises after activation, and the changing pressure gradients then determine valve closure and opening.

AV valve closure is therefore a mechanical consequence associated with the early systolic sequence, not the definition of the QRS complex. Atrial depolarization produces the P wave, while ventricular repolarization produces the T wave. Atrial repolarization usually occurs during the larger ventricular signal and is not clearly seen as a separate wave. For interpretation, keep the layers separate: QRS identifies an electrical event; pressure traces identify force-related chamber changes; valve events follow pressure crossings. This separation prevents the common mistake of treating an ECG as a direct record of ejection or heart sounds.

## w07-mc15

The **T wave reflects ventricular repolarization**, making **A** correct. During repolarization, membrane currents return ventricular cells toward their resting electrical state. Calcium handling and cross-bridge processes then allow the myocardium to relax as the cycle progresses.

Electrical and mechanical events overlap rather than occurring as perfectly separate blocks, so the T wave is not a direct measurement of ventricular relaxation or filling volume. It occurs toward the later part of the ventricular electrical cycle, not at the initial atrial depolarization represented by P. SA nodal firing initiates the sequence but is not itself what T represents. The direction of the T-wave deflection also cannot be interpreted simply as the membrane voltage of one cell: an ECG is a spatially summed surface recording. Identify the event first, then relate its timing to the pressure and volume changes on a cardiac-cycle diagram.

## w07-mc16

An ECG records **electrical potential differences between body-surface electrodes** generated by the heart's changing electrical activity. This makes **A** correct. Its waves summarize activity across many cells rather than tracing the membrane potential of a single ventricular myocyte.

Because electrical excitation triggers contraction, ECG timing can help infer when mechanical events are likely to occur. However, ventricular pressure, stroke volume, and valve area require other measurements; they are not directly recorded by the ECG. A normal-looking electrical sequence therefore does not by itself establish normal pumping performance. Likewise, the height of an ECG wave should not be treated as a direct measure of chamber pressure. The important distinction is **direct observation versus physiological inference**: the instrument measures electrical differences, while interpretation may connect those differences to conduction, rhythm, and the expected sequence of cardiac mechanics.

## w07-mc17

Isovolumetric contraction starts after rising ventricular pressure has closed the AV valves but before ventricular pressure is high enough to open the semilunar valves. With both inlet and outlet valves closed, the ventricle develops pressure without changing its contained blood volume. **A** is therefore correct.

The term isovolumetric describes constant **volume**, not constant pressure or lack of contraction. Pressure rises steeply because the muscle is generating force around a closed chamber. Once ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the aorta or pulmonary artery, the corresponding semilunar valve opens and ejection begins. An increase in ventricular volume would instead require inflow, while falling pressure with both valves closed describes isovolumetric relaxation. When reading a pressure–volume loop, this contraction phase appears as an upward movement at essentially fixed end-diastolic volume.

## w07-mc18

The aortic valve separates the left ventricle from the aorta. It opens when **left-ventricular pressure becomes greater than aortic pressure**, producing a forward pressure gradient. **A** is correct, and valve opening marks the start of ventricular ejection.

Before that crossing, ventricular pressure may already be rising rapidly, but the aortic valve remains closed and the ventricle is in isovolumetric contraction. If aortic pressure exceeds ventricular pressure, the gradient favors closure rather than forward opening. Atrial pressure exceeding ventricular pressure concerns the mitral valve, not the aortic valve. The P wave marks atrial depolarization and is too indirect to define aortic opening. On a Wiggers diagram, find the ventricular and aortic pressure traces and locate their relevant crossing; do not assign the event solely from an ECG landmark.

## w07-mc19

The mitral valve opens when **left-atrial pressure exceeds left-ventricular pressure**. This normally occurs as the ventricle relaxes and its pressure falls sufficiently low. Blood can then enter from the atrium, beginning ventricular filling. Thus **A** is correct.

During the preceding isovolumetric relaxation phase, both the mitral and aortic valves are closed. Ventricular pressure is falling, but ventricular volume remains near end-systolic volume until the mitral valve opens. If ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the relevant event is aortic opening and ejection, not mitral opening. A peak in aortic pressure or the start of QRS does not override the pressure difference across the mitral valve. Always identify the two chambers or vessels connected by the valve. For the mitral valve, the useful comparison is atrium versus ventricle.

## w07-mc20

**Stroke volume is the amount ejected by one ventricle in one beat.** The ventricle starts systole with its end-diastolic volume and retains its end-systolic volume afterward. Subtracting what remains from what was present gives SV = EDV − ESV = 130 − 50 = **80 mL per beat**, so **B** is correct.

The 50 mL value is the volume remaining after ejection, not the volume ejected. The 130 mL value is the filled volume before contraction. Adding the two volumes has no useful meaning for this calculation. As a check, the result should be positive and smaller than EDV under these conditions. This calculation does not yet give cardiac output because no heart rate has been supplied. Keep the per-beat volume separate from the per-minute flow obtained by multiplying stroke volume by heart rate.

## w07-mc21

Cardiac output is the volume pumped by one ventricle each minute. Multiply the number of beats per minute by the volume ejected in each beat: CO = HR × SV = 75 beats/min × 80 mL/beat = **6,000 mL/min**, or **6.0 L/min**. The beat units cancel, making **B** correct.

The factor of 1,000 between milliliters and liters is the main arithmetic trap. A result of 0.6 or 60 L/min would be a tenfold conversion error. Adding heart rate and stroke volume is also invalid because their units describe different quantities. The two ventricles normally pump approximately the same average flow in series; cardiac output is not doubled by adding their outputs. Writing the units alongside each number both explains the physiology and provides a check on the calculation.

## w07-mc22

The **Frank–Starling mechanism** links ventricular filling to the force of the subsequent contraction. Within the normal operating range, a higher EDV increases end-diastolic myocardial stretch, which enhances force generation and tends to increase stroke volume. **A** is therefore correct.

This is an intrinsic length-dependent response that helps the heart pump the blood returning to it. It does not require the SA node to stop or an immediate increase in sympathetic input. Nor does it mean that every degree of stretch produces unlimited improvement: the phrase **within physiological limits** is essential. Distinguish this mechanism from positive inotropy, which increases contractile performance at a given preload through changes such as altered calcium handling. Here the initiating change is greater filling, not a new contractility state. On a Starling graph, that corresponds to movement along a relationship rather than necessarily shifting the relationship upward.

## w07-mc23

**Chronotropy refers to heart rate**, so a positive chronotropic effect increases the frequency of beats. This makes **A** correct. For example, a change in pacemaker activity that brings SA nodal cells to threshold more frequently can shorten the interval between beats.

Increasing force at the same initial fiber length describes positive inotropy instead. A stimulus such as sympathetic activation can produce both positive chronotropic and positive inotropic effects, but the two terms identify different properties. Blood viscosity and venous return affect cardiovascular function through other mechanisms and do not define chronotropy. To avoid mixing the terms, ask which variable changes: beats per minute indicates chronotropy; contractile performance at a given loading condition indicates inotropy. Their combined consequences for output can then be analyzed using CO = HR × SV.

## w07-mc24

A **positive inotropic effect** increases myocardial contractility: the muscle can develop greater force and eject more effectively at a given preload and comparable loading conditions. **A** is correct. Sympathetic beta1 signaling is an important example because it enhances calcium handling within ventricular myocytes.

This differs from the Frank–Starling response, where greater filling increases force through length-dependent activation without necessarily changing the underlying contractility state. It also differs from chronotropy, which concerns heart rate. A positive inotropic effect can lower ESV and increase SV when other conditions are comparable, but those consequences should not be treated as independent of afterload and filling. Reduced blood volume or sensory firing does not define inotropy. The important comparison is **stronger contraction at the same preload**, not simply any circumstance in which more blood happens to be pumped.

## w07-mc25

Sympathetic stimulation activates beta1 receptors on ventricular myocytes, increasing intracellular signaling that enhances calcium entry and SR calcium cycling. More activating calcium can be made available to the contractile machinery, producing stronger contractions. This makes **A** correct.

The response is not simply to leave calcium elevated forever. Enhanced SR uptake also helps relaxation and replenishes calcium available for later release, supporting repeated faster and stronger beats. Blocking all L-type calcium channels would remove an important trigger for contraction rather than explain increased contractility. Removing troponin would disrupt thin-filament regulation. Permanently reducing SR calcium uptake would impair relaxation and normal cycling. A complete explanation therefore links sympathetic signaling to **controlled enhancement of calcium entry, release, and reuptake**, not merely to a vague statement that more sympathetic activity makes the heart work harder.

## w07-mc26

The first heart sound, **S1**, is associated with closure of the atrioventricular valves near the start of ventricular systole. Rising ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure, the mitral and tricuspid valves close, and vibrations associated with this abrupt mechanical event contribute to the sound. **A** is correct.

S1 does not mean that the semilunar valves have already opened. A brief isovolumetric contraction phase follows AV closure before ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure and ejection begins. Atrial depolarization is the P-wave electrical event, while closure of the semilunar valves is associated with S2. Avoid treating the sounds as equivalent to ECG waves or to valves actively contracting. Use a pressure-based sequence: ventricular pressure rises, AV valves close, S1 occurs around that event, pressure continues rising, and semilunar opening follows when the appropriate gradient develops.

## w07-mc27

**End-systolic volume**, or ESV, is the blood remaining in a ventricle after ejection has ended. This makes **B** correct. The ventricle normally does not empty completely with each beat; the retained volume is an essential part of the stroke-volume calculation.

End-diastolic volume is the amount present after filling and before the next ventricular contraction. Their difference gives stroke volume: SV = EDV − ESV. Blood entering an atrium per minute is a flow rate, not a ventricular volume at a defined point in the cycle. A P-wave landmark likewise does not define ESV. On a ventricular-volume trace, ESV lies at the low-volume end of ejection and is maintained through isovolumetric relaxation until filling begins. The prefix and timing matter: end-systolic refers to what is left after the pumping phase, not what was present before it.

## w07-mc28

The relationship CO = HR × SV tells you how the two quantities combine. Under the question's explicit assumption that **stroke volume stays unchanged**, a higher heart rate means more identical stroke volumes are ejected each minute. Cardiac output therefore rises, so **A** is correct.

The assumption is important. In an intact person, a large increase in heart rate can shorten filling time and alter stroke volume, so cardiac output does not necessarily increase without limit. That more complicated situation is not what this question specifies. For a numerical check, holding SV at 70 mL/beat while increasing HR from 60 to 80 beats/min changes CO from 4.2 to 5.6 L/min. The reasoning is conditional rather than a universal claim about every tachycardia. First use the variables held constant, then consider physiological qualifications.

## w07-sr01

Cardiac contractile myocytes are relatively short, branched, striated cells, usually with a central nucleus. Intercalated discs provide mechanical attachments and gap-junction electrical coupling. Skeletal muscle fibers are long, multinucleated cells, and each fiber is activated at its own neuromuscular junction rather than being excited by current passing directly from neighboring fibers.

Both tissues use actin, myosin, troponin, tropomyosin, and calcium-regulated cross-bridge cycling, but their activation differs. In skeletal muscle, T-tubule voltage sensors are directly coupled to SR calcium-release channels. In ventricular muscle, extracellular calcium entering through L-type channels triggers further SR release. Cardiac contraction therefore depends importantly on this extracellular trigger calcium as well as the intracellular store.

Connect these differences to function. Cardiac electrical coupling supports coordinated chamber contraction, and a prolonged action potential and refractory period normally prevent tetanus, preserving relaxation and filling. Skeletal muscle can vary force through motor-unit recruitment and frequency summation because its electrical refractory period is brief compared with the twitch. A strong comparison explains not only how the cells look, but how their coupling and calcium mechanisms suit the heart's rhythmic pumping and skeletal muscle's flexible force control.

## w07-sr02

A ventricular action potential depolarizes the sarcolemma and T-tubule membrane, opening L-type calcium channels. Calcium entering through these channels activates nearby ryanodine receptors in the SR. A larger amount of stored calcium is then released into the cytosol: **calcium-induced calcium release**.

The cytosolic calcium rise activates troponin, allowing tropomyosin to move away from inhibitory positions on actin. ATP-dependent myosin cycling generates force. When the calcium signal declines, calcium leaves troponin and thin-filament inhibition returns. SERCA pumps calcium into the SR, while sodium–calcium exchange and other membrane transport mechanisms help extrude calcium from the cell.

The explanation should include both activation and recovery because the ventricle must repeat the cycle, not remain contracted. Extracellular trigger calcium and SR release are separate but linked stages; neither alone describes the complete mechanism. Also distinguish the energetic processes: SERCA uses ATP directly, while sodium–calcium exchange depends on the sodium gradient. This pathway explains why a cardiac action potential can alter contraction strength through calcium handling and why normal relaxation requires calcium removal rather than merely completion of the ECG signal.

## w07-sr03

A red blood cell arriving through a vena cava enters the **right atrium**, passes through the **tricuspid valve** into the **right ventricle**, and is ejected through the **pulmonary valve** into the pulmonary arteries. It passes through pulmonary capillaries, where gas exchange with the lungs occurs. It then returns through the pulmonary veins to the **left atrium**, crosses the **mitral valve** into the **left ventricle**, and is ejected through the **aortic valve** into the aorta.

The sequence demonstrates that the pulmonary and systemic circuits operate in series. The right side sends blood to the lungs; the left side sends blood to the systemic circulation. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins return it, so the pulmonary vessels are not named according to whether their blood is oxygen-rich.

For a complete answer, include both valve sets and do not jump directly from one ventricle to the other. Valves support one-way movement by responding to pressure gradients. The chambers do not normally mix right- and left-sided blood through a direct open connection. Trace the cell continuously and check that every named valve lies between the two structures you have connected.

## w07-sr04

The ventricular action potential begins with a rapid depolarization driven largely by fast sodium entry. Sodium channels then inactivate, and early outward currents contribute to a brief initial repolarization. During the **plateau**, inward L-type calcium current partly balances outward potassium current, keeping the membrane depolarized. As inward calcium current declines and outward potassium effects predominate, the cell repolarizes toward its resting potential.

The plateau is physiologically important for two linked reasons. Calcium entry helps trigger SR calcium release and contraction, and prolonged depolarization prolongs the refractory period. This refractory period overlaps much of the mechanical response, preventing the rapid repeated activation needed to summate contractions into tetanus. The ventricle can therefore relax and refill between beats.

Do not say that a flat plateau means no ions are moving; it reflects a changing balance of currents. Also distinguish this waveform from the SA nodal action potential, which has an unstable diastolic potential and a calcium-dependent upstroke. A strong answer connects the ionic sequence, the shape of the trace, and the pumping consequence rather than simply naming phases without explaining their function.

## w07-sr05

SA nodal cells do not remain at a stable resting potential. After repolarization, their membrane gradually depolarizes during diastole because of the combined effects of the funny current and changing potassium and calcium conductances. When threshold is reached, calcium-channel activity supports the nodal action-potential upstroke. Potassium efflux then repolarizes the membrane and the cycle begins again.

Sympathetic beta1 signaling promotes more rapid progression toward threshold, shortening the interval between action potentials and raising heart rate. Parasympathetic ACh acting through muscarinic receptors reduces pacemaker drive and increases potassium conductance, tending to make the membrane more negative and slow the approach to threshold. This lowers heart rate.

The question asks for a causal account of automaticity and its modulation. Saying that the autonomic nervous system "starts every heartbeat" would be misleading: pacemaker cells can generate a rhythm intrinsically, while autonomic input adjusts it. Nor is If the only current involved. Explain the unstable diastolic potential, threshold crossing, repolarization, and how changing the timing of those stages changes the number of beats per minute.

## w07-sr06

Normal cardiac excitation begins in the **SA node** and spreads through atrial myocardium. It reaches the **AV node**, where conduction slows. The impulse then travels through the **AV bundle (His bundle)**, right and left bundle branches, and **Purkinje fibers**, which distribute activation rapidly through ventricular myocardium.

The slow AV nodal stage is useful rather than an accidental inefficiency. It allows atrial activation and contraction to occur before ventricular contraction raises pressure and closes the AV valves. Atrial contraction completes ventricular filling after the earlier passive filling phase. Rapid downstream distribution then helps the ventricles contract in an organized sequence suited to ejection.

Keep the two different timing properties separate: the SA node sets the normal initiating rhythm through automaticity, while conduction pathways determine how quickly excitation reaches different regions. This is also not the route taken by blood. A good diagram can show electrical arrows across the atria and into the ventricular conduction system, with the AV node marked as the delay point. The explanation should connect every anatomical stage to coordinated pumping, not merely present an ordered list of names.

## w07-sr07

The **P wave** represents atrial depolarization. Atrial contraction follows and contributes to the final part of ventricular filling. The **QRS complex** represents ventricular depolarization. Ventricular force and pressure then rise, the AV valves close, and—after isovolumetric contraction—the semilunar valves open when ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure. The **T wave** represents ventricular repolarization and occurs toward the later part of systole as the system moves toward relaxation.

The central distinction is that the ECG is electrical, whereas contraction, pressure changes, valve movement, and blood flow are mechanical consequences. They are linked but not identical or perfectly simultaneous. Valve events depend on pressure gradients rather than a wave directly opening or closing a leaflet.

On a Wiggers diagram, use P, QRS, and T as timing landmarks and then read the pressure and volume curves separately. Atrial repolarization is usually obscured by the larger QRS signal. Do not label the P wave as atrial force, QRS as stroke volume, or T as a direct measurement of ventricular filling. The answer should explain the causal order and acknowledge the overlap of electrical and mechanical events.

## w07-sr08

## w07-sr09

The word **isovolumetric** means that ventricular blood volume remains essentially constant. In isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure has risen above atrial pressure and closed the AV valves, but it has not yet exceeded arterial pressure enough to open the semilunar valves. With inlet and outlet closed, muscle contraction raises pressure around a nearly fixed volume of blood.

In isovolumetric relaxation, ventricular pressure has fallen below arterial pressure and the semilunar valves have closed. It remains above atrial pressure briefly, so the AV valves are still closed. The relaxing myocardium lowers pressure, but no filling or ejection occurs during that interval.

The two phases thus share the same valve configuration but differ in the direction of pressure change and the volume at which they occur. Contraction is near **EDV**; relaxation is near **ESV**. On a pressure–volume loop, they form the approximately vertical sides. Do not infer that constant volume means no muscle activity or no energy use. It means that pressure can change even without net blood entering or leaving the chamber.

## w07-sr10

**EDV** is the volume in a ventricle at the end of filling, immediately before its systolic contraction. **ESV** is the volume remaining after ejection. Their difference is **stroke volume**, the amount ejected by one ventricle per beat: SV = EDV − ESV. **Heart rate** is beats per minute. **Cardiac output** is the volume pumped per minute: CO = HR × SV.

For example, EDV 130 mL and ESV 50 mL give SV 80 mL/beat. At 75 beats/min, CO is 6,000 mL/min, or 6.0 L/min. Writing the units shows why subtraction is appropriate for the two volumes and multiplication is appropriate for heart rate and stroke volume. Beat units cancel in the output calculation.

Do not confuse what remains with what is ejected, and do not add the outputs of the two ventricles as though they supply separate parallel flows. In steady state, they pump approximately the same average flow through circuits in series. A complete answer defines the quantities in physiological time, provides both equations, and distinguishes volume per beat from volume flow per minute.

## w07-sr11

The **Frank–Starling relationship** describes an intrinsic response to increased filling. Greater venous return can increase EDV and myocardial stretch. Within the physiological range, that length-dependent activation allows the ventricle to generate greater force and eject a larger stroke volume. This helps output match the blood arriving at the heart without requiring a new neural command for every change in filling.

**Sympathetic inotropy** changes contractile performance at a given preload, largely through beta1-mediated changes in calcium entry and SR handling. At the same starting EDV and comparable afterload, a more contractile ventricle can eject more and leave a smaller ESV. On a Starling-style graph, increased filling moves the operating point along a curve, while increased contractility can shift the relationship upward.

Both can increase stroke volume, but the initiating variables differ. Do not define every increase in contraction force as increased contractility: a stronger beat caused solely by greater initial stretch is the Frank–Starling response. ## w07-sr12

A beta1-adrenergic agonist acts on both pacemaker and contractile tissues. In the **SA node**, beta1 signaling promotes faster spontaneous depolarization toward threshold, producing a positive chronotropic effect: heart rate rises. In **ventricular myocytes**, enhanced calcium entry and SR calcium cycling increase contractility, a positive inotropic effect.

At comparable filling and afterload, stronger ventricular contraction ejects more blood, so ESV tends to decrease. Because SV = EDV − ESV, lowering ESV at a given EDV raises stroke volume. With increases in HR and SV, CO = HR × SV predicts a rise in cardiac output. Enhanced calcium reuptake also supports relaxation between beats rather than leaving the myocardium permanently activated.

State the assumptions rather than treating the prediction as unconditional. A very high heart rate can shorten filling time, and changes in venous return or afterload can modify stroke volume. The expected course-level response is increased rate and contractility, lower ESV, and increased SV and CO under comparable loading conditions. A complete answer distinguishes the nodal effect from the ventricular effect and uses the equations to connect them to output.
