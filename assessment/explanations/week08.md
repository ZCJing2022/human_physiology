# Week 8 explanation narratives

## w08-mc01

**Arterioles** are major adjustable resistance vessels because their smooth muscle can change lumen radius substantially. A change in radius has a strong effect on resistance, so constriction or dilation can alter flow into a capillary bed and contribute to changes in total peripheral resistance. This makes the arteriole choice correct.

Large elastic arteries are especially important as pressure conduits and reservoirs. Capillaries provide extensive thin-walled exchange surfaces, while veins have high compliance and serve as major blood-volume reservoirs. These roles overlap within an integrated circulation, but they are not interchangeable. The question asks where active adjustment of resistance is especially important, not which vessel contains the most blood or has the smallest individual diameter. Many capillaries in parallel have a large combined cross-sectional area. Focus on the combination of small vessel radius and adjustable smooth-muscle tone in arterioles.

## w08-mc02

The relationship Q = ΔP/R states that volume flow increases with the **pressure difference** driving it and decreases with resistance. If resistance is fixed and the pressure gradient increases, flow must increase. Thus **A** is correct.

For example, doubling the pressure difference across an unchanged resistance would double flow in this simplified relationship. The relevant pressure is the difference between upstream and downstream values, not the absolute upstream pressure alone. Raising both pressures by the same amount would not change their difference. Flow reverses only if the direction of the gradient reverses; a larger gradient in the same direction does not automatically reverse flow. The unchanged-resistance condition also matters because real vessels can alter their tone. Apply the stated assumptions first, and then distinguish the direct physical effect from any later regulatory response.

## w08-mc03

Vascular resistance depends strongly on vessel radius. In the idealized Poiseuille relationship, resistance is proportional to **1/radius⁴** when length and viscosity are held constant. A decrease in radius therefore increases resistance steeply, making **A** correct.

As an illustration, reducing radius to one-half would increase resistance sixteenfold in that idealized model. For a fixed pressure gradient, Q = ΔP/R then predicts a large fall in flow. The calculation explains why relatively small changes in arteriolar smooth-muscle tone can strongly influence perfusion. Actual blood vessels are living, branching, compliant structures, so the ideal model is a conceptual approximation rather than a guarantee of an exact whole-body response. Length and viscosity still matter; changing radius does not abolish their effects. Separate the controlled variable, the resulting resistance change, and the conditional effect on flow.

## w08-mc04

Average linear velocity is related to volume flow divided by the **total cross-sectional area** available at that level of the circulation. The capillary network contains an enormous number of vessels in parallel, giving it a much greater combined area than the aorta. For the same overall flow through the circuit, average velocity is therefore lowest in capillaries. **B** is correct.

The counterintuitive point is that each capillary is narrow, but the relevant comparison is the sum of the areas of all parallel capillaries, not one capillary versus the aorta. Slow transit supports exchange by providing time for substances to move between blood and tissue. Low velocity does not mean that capillaries receive no flow or that the volume flow through the entire systemic circuit disappears. Distinguish **velocity, distance per time**, from **flow rate, volume per time**.

## w08-mc05

Many small substances cross capillary walls by **diffusion down concentration gradients**, so **A** is correct. Oxygen and carbon dioxide can diffuse through cell membranes, while the routes available to water-soluble solutes depend on the capillary's permeability and structural type. The direction of movement follows the relevant concentration difference.

This is different from bulk fluid movement driven by hydrostatic and colloid osmotic forces. Filtration and absorption concern movement of fluid across the wall; diffusion explains much of the exchange of individual dissolved substances. The two processes can occur together but should not be treated as synonyms. Skeletal muscle contraction can assist venous return, action potentials transmit electrical information, and coagulation forms a clot after vascular injury. None is the principal mechanism by which small exchange solutes cross a functioning capillary wall. Identify whether the question asks about solute diffusion or bulk water movement.

## w08-mc06

Capillary filtration moves fluid into the interstitial space, and some plasma proteins also escape the vascular compartment. The **lymphatic system collects excess interstitial fluid and proteins and returns them to the blood**, making **A** correct. This helps maintain tissue-fluid balance rather than allowing filtered material to accumulate indefinitely.

Lymph return is especially important for proteins, which are not simply recovered everywhere by reversing their diffusion across blood capillaries. If filtration exceeds lymphatic drainage, or drainage is obstructed, edema can develop. The lymphatics do not generate the normal SA nodal rhythm, provide ACh release at neuromuscular junctions, or produce sound waves. Connect their role to the exchange circulation: capillaries deliver and exchange materials, while lymphatics provide a return route for excess filtered fluid and macromolecules. Neither system's function is adequately described by the vague phrase "moving blood around."

## w08-mc07

Large elastic arteries expand as the ventricle ejects blood, storing some of that mechanical energy in their stretched walls. During diastole, **elastic recoil** continues to support arterial pressure and forward flow even while the ventricle is not ejecting. This makes **A** correct.

The artery acts as a pressure reservoir that smooths the intermittent output of the heart. It does not eliminate the systolic pressure rise; expansion and recoil are responses to the pulsatile input. Nor does arterial recoil directly open the AV valves, whose position depends on atrial–ventricular pressure differences. Erythrocyte production occurs mainly in bone marrow, not elastic arterial walls. The useful comparison is between a pulsatile pump and a more continuous downstream flow. Arterial compliance and recoil help bridge the time between successive ventricular ejections.

## w08-mc08

First calculate pulse pressure: SBP − DBP = 120 − 75 = **45 mmHg**. At an ordinary resting heart rate, approximate MAP as DBP + one-third pulse pressure: 75 + 45/3 = **90 mmHg**. Therefore **B** is correct.

MAP is a time-weighted average over the cardiac cycle, not simply the midpoint between systolic and diastolic pressure. Because diastole ordinarily occupies more time than systole, the average lies closer to the diastolic value. The simple arithmetic mean here would be 97.5 mmHg and would not use the course approximation. Adding the pressures gives no meaningful MAP estimate. The one-third formula is itself an approximation that becomes less reliable when heart rate or waveform timing changes substantially. Show the subtraction, division, addition, and pressure units so the reasoning is reproducible.

## w08-mc09

Mean arterial pressure reflects the relationship between the flow generated by the heart and the resistance of the systemic circulation. In the usual simplified form, **MAP ≈ CO × TPR**, making **A** the useful relationship. More precisely, the pressure difference from systemic arteries to the right atrium is related to cardiac output times systemic resistance; right-atrial pressure is often small enough to neglect in this introductory approximation.

Because CO = HR × SV, changes in heart rate or stroke volume can influence MAP. Arteriolar tone alters total peripheral resistance. The other expressions confuse MAP with stroke volume, incorrectly divide heart rate by stroke volume, or combine unrelated blood components. Use the relationship to organize a causal explanation, while remembering that compensatory changes in one variable can modify the effect of a change in another. It is not a claim that all variables remain independent in a living person.

## w08-mc10

A loss of circulating volume reduces the filling available to the heart. Venous return tends to fall, lowering ventricular EDV. Through the Frank–Starling relationship, reduced filling tends to reduce stroke volume; if this lowers cardiac output, MAP also falls. Thus **A** identifies the main immediate chain.

Write it explicitly: **volume loss → less venous return → lower EDV → lower SV → lower CO → lower MAP**. Reflex increases in heart rate, contractility, and vascular tone may subsequently compensate, so the eventual measured pressure depends on both the disturbance and the response. The question asks about the initial tendency, not a guarantee that every volume loss produces the same final pressure. Phototransduction and nicotinic receptors are unrelated to this primary hemodynamic pathway. Volume loss has a real circulatory effect, not merely a change in plasma protein concentration with no effect on filling.

## w08-mc11

**Myogenic autoregulation** is a local response of vascular smooth muscle to stretch. When increased local pressure stretches an arteriole, the muscle can respond by contracting, narrowing the lumen and raising resistance. When stretch falls, relaxation can reduce resistance. This makes **A** correct.

The response can buffer changes in tissue flow: a pressure increase would otherwise raise Q, but the associated resistance increase opposes part of that rise. It is therefore not simply the passive stretching of a tube, which initially tends to widen the vessel. Nor does it require a somatic motor neuron releasing ACh at a neuromuscular junction. Myogenic means that the response originates in the muscle's own properties. Light detection and fibrin production concern other systems. Distinguish this local pressure-related response from metabolic vasodilation, which adjusts flow in relation to tissue demand and chemical conditions.

## w08-mc12

An active tissue consumes more oxygen and changes its local chemical environment. Metabolites and paracrine signals can relax nearby arteriolar smooth muscle, producing **vasodilation** and increased perfusion. This matching of blood supply to metabolic activity makes **A** correct.

The immediate effect is a decrease in local resistance. For an available driving pressure, Q = ΔP/R predicts greater flow into that vascular bed. The response is called active hyperemia when it accompanies increased metabolic activity. It should not be confused with reactive hyperemia, the increased flow after a temporary restriction is released. Constriction alone would generally oppose the increased delivery needed by an active tissue, while clotting would obstruct flow. Ventricular repolarization is an electrical cardiac event, not the local mechanism. Local metabolic control can interact with sympathetic tone, so tissue flow reflects competing influences rather than one universal command.

## w08-mc13

Norepinephrine released by sympathetic neurons commonly activates **alpha1 adrenergic receptors** on systemic arteriolar smooth muscle. The resulting signaling increases smooth-muscle contraction, narrows the lumen, and raises resistance. Thus **A**, vasoconstriction, is correct.

This can redistribute flow and contribute to maintaining arterial pressure. It does not imply that every vessel responds identically to every catecholamine. Receptor distribution and ligand matter: beta2-mediated effects of circulating epinephrine in certain vascular beds can favor dilation. The question specifically names norepinephrine acting at alpha1 receptors, so the alpha1 response is the relevant one. Platelet destruction is unrelated. To explain rather than memorize the answer, trace **receptor activation → muscle contraction → reduced radius → increased resistance**. Then distinguish the local flow consequence from the overall effect on total peripheral resistance and MAP.

## w08-mc14

The major high-pressure arterial baroreceptors are located in the **carotid sinus and aortic arch**, making **A** correct. These sensory endings respond to stretch of the arterial wall, providing rapid information about pressure-related changes to brainstem cardiovascular circuits.

They are not the same as the carotid bodies, which are important peripheral chemoreceptors. Nor should the named arterial sensors be confused with lower-pressure cardiopulmonary receptors elsewhere in the circulation. Carotid sinus afferent information travels principally through the glossopharyngeal pathway, while aortic arch afferents travel through the vagus. The cochlea is an auditory organ and cannot serve as the arterial pressure sensor. A complete explanation identifies location, detected physical variable, and sensory output: arterial wall stretch changes firing, which helps the CNS adjust cardiac and vascular autonomic activity.

## w08-mc15

A sudden fall in MAP usually reduces stretch of the carotid sinus and aortic arch walls. Their stretch-sensitive baroreceptor endings then fire **less frequently**, so **A** is correct. The sensor response should be established before discussing the compensatory autonomic response.

Reduced afferent input changes brainstem cardiovascular activity, leading to increased sympathetic drive and reduced parasympathetic influence on the heart. These output changes can raise heart rate, contractility, and vascular tone. It is easy to confuse increased sympathetic firing with increased baroreceptor firing, but they lie on different sides of the reflex: the afferent sensor signal falls while compensatory sympathetic output rises. The receptors do not turn into motor neurons or remain fixed in their firing. The reflex works because the CNS interprets the change in sensory input and adjusts effectors in the opposing direction.

## w08-mc16

When MAP falls, compensatory sympathetic activation and parasympathetic withdrawal can increase heart rate and contractility. Sympathetic arteriolar constriction increases total peripheral resistance, and venous constriction can improve venous return. **A** includes two responses that help restore pressure: increased HR and arteriolar vasoconstriction.

Use MAP ≈ CO × TPR and CO = HR × SV to connect the response to its purpose. Increasing cardiac output and resistance can oppose the initial pressure fall. Decreasing heart rate, reducing contractility, or dilating vessels would generally worsen that immediate tendency rather than compensate for it. The reflex does not necessarily restore pressure completely, especially with severe volume loss, and very high HR can compromise filling. Nevertheless, the direction of the normal short-term response is clear. Distinguish the mechanism that helps oppose a disturbance from a guarantee that compensation always succeeds.

## w08-mc17

Vomiting and diarrhea can remove fluid from the body and reduce circulating plasma volume. Less available circulating volume can reduce venous return and ventricular filling, lowering stroke volume and cardiac output. If arterial pressure and cerebral perfusion fall sufficiently, consciousness may be lost. This chain makes **A** correct in the supplied tutorial scenario.

Standing still can add a further challenge because gravity favors venous pooling and an inactive skeletal-muscle pump provides less assistance to venous return. The baroreflex may compensate, but severe depletion can exceed its ability to maintain adequate perfusion. New red-cell production takes far longer than minutes and does not explain the immediate event. Rod hyperpolarization is unrelated, and dehydration does not invariably raise MAP. This is a physiological explanation of the course case, not a claim that every episode of fainting has the same cause.

## w08-mc18

**Water** is the largest component of plasma by volume, so **A** is correct. Plasma is the liquid extracellular component in which proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, dissolved gases, and wastes are carried. Its water content provides the solvent and contributes to circulating volume.

Albumin is the most abundant plasma protein, but that does not make it the largest component of plasma overall. Red blood cells and platelets are formed elements suspended in plasma rather than constituents of the plasma fraction itself. After centrifugation, these distinctions become visible as separate fractions. The question therefore tests two classification levels: what makes up plasma, and how plasma differs from the cellular and platelet components of whole blood. Read the phrase **by volume** carefully and do not substitute a correct fact about protein abundance for the broader question about the principal liquid component.

## w08-mc19

**Albumin** is the most abundant plasma protein under ordinary conditions, making **A** correct. It contributes substantially to plasma colloid osmotic pressure and also carries various substances in the circulation. Its abundance and confinement largely within the vascular compartment are important to fluid exchange.

Hemoglobin is abundant in whole blood but is normally contained inside red blood cells rather than being the dominant freely circulating plasma protein. Fibrin is the insoluble material generated during clot formation; soluble fibrinogen is a plasma clotting protein but is not the most abundant plasma protein. Myosin is an intracellular motor protein associated with contraction. Distinguish the liquid plasma compartment from the formed elements and from a formed clot. A fact about the abundance of a protein somewhere in blood does not automatically make it a fact about plasma.

## w08-mc20

**Hematopoiesis** means production and differentiation of blood cells and their lineages, so **A** is correct. In adults, this occurs mainly in bone marrow, where stem and progenitor cells give rise to erythrocytes, different leukocyte populations, and megakaryocytes that produce platelets.

The similar-looking alternatives describe different processes. Hemostasis is the stopping of bleeding through vascular, platelet, and coagulation responses. Homeostasis is the broader maintenance of regulated internal conditions. Hemolysis is destruction of red blood cells. The shared word roots can make these easy to confuse, but the physiological distinction is straightforward: hematopoiesis **produces blood elements**, hemostasis **limits blood loss**, and hemolysis **breaks down erythrocytes**. A correct explanation should also recognize regulation by signaling molecules such as erythropoietin and other cytokines rather than treating cell production as a fixed unregulated output.

## w08-mc21

Reduced oxygen availability in the kidneys stimulates increased **erythropoietin**, or EPO, production. EPO acts on erythroid precursors in bone marrow and promotes increased red-cell production. **A** is therefore correct.

This is a negative-feedback response to an oxygen-delivery problem: a greater red-cell mass can increase the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity and reduce the original hypoxic signal. The response develops over days rather than seconds, because new cells must be produced and mature. It is not the immediate mechanism that restores pressure through a baroreflex. Sound intensity, rod cGMP, and platelet aggregation do not define the renal trigger. Also distinguish the site of hormone production from the site of cell production: the kidneys provide much of the EPO signal, while the marrow supplies the developing erythrocytes. The kidney does not simply release newly formed red cells into blood as its EPO response.

## w08-mc22

Mature human erythrocytes lack a nucleus and are packed with **hemoglobin**, which binds oxygen. This specialization, together with their biconcave shape and flexibility, supports gas transport and passage through narrow capillaries. **A** is correct.

Mature red cells also lack mitochondria, so the option proposing many mitochondria is not an explanation of their normal oxygen-transport role. They obtain ATP through glycolysis rather than using mitochondria to consume the oxygen they carry. Sarcomeres are contractile assemblies of muscle, and ACh secretion is not a defining erythrocyte function. The absence of a nucleus does not make the cell inert: it still needs membrane integrity, ion regulation, and metabolic support. The point is structural specialization for a limited task, not the absence of all cellular activity. Distinguish the abundant intracellular hemoglobin from proteins dissolved in plasma.

## w08-mc23

The central consequence of anemia is reduced **oxygen-carrying capacity**, usually reflecting insufficient hemoglobin concentration or red-cell mass for the relevant context. Less hemoglobin is available to carry oxygen per unit volume of blood, making **A** correct.

This is not identical to a low arterial oxygen partial pressure. Oxygen pressure and hemoglobin saturation can be relatively normal while total oxygen content is reduced because there is less hemoglobin available. That distinction explains why simply noting that oxygen is present in the lungs does not establish normal tissue oxygen delivery. Hearing acuity, nicotinic-receptor density, and plasma water content are not the defining consequences. Plasma volume changes can affect measured concentrations, but anemia cannot be reduced to a universal statement about water content. Link the blood component to function: hemoglobin carries oxygen, so a deficit compromises transport even when other parts of the pathway work.

## w08-mc24

After vascular injury exposes a normally protected subendothelial surface, platelets **adhere and become activated**. Activated platelets change shape, release signaling molecules, and become more able to bind one another. This initiates and amplifies formation of a platelet plug, making **A** correct.

Adhesion means attaching at the injured surface; aggregation means platelets attaching to one another. They are related stages, not interchangeable words. Coagulation subsequently produces fibrin that reinforces the plug, although these processes overlap in time. Ventricular ejection, rhodopsin activation, and motor-unit recruitment concern unrelated functions. The question asks for an early platelet response, so do not begin with the final mature fibrin clot and omit how platelets first localize to the injury. A useful explanation follows exposure of the adhesive surface, platelet attachment, activation, recruitment, and later stabilization.

## w08-mc25

In the introductory coagulation scheme, intrinsic and extrinsic pathways feed into a common pathway that produces **thrombin**. Thrombin converts soluble **fibrinogen into fibrin**, and the resulting fibrin network helps stabilize the platelet plug. Therefore **A** is correct.

The cascade amplifies the response because activated clotting factors help activate additional factors. The intrinsic/extrinsic diagram is a useful organizational model, while coagulation in the body is an interacting process rather than two completely isolated systems. Distinguish thrombin, an enzyme, from fibrin, the structural product, and distinguish fibrin from its soluble precursor fibrinogen. ACh, rhodopsin, cGMP, and EPO belong to other pathways. Platelets provide an early plug and surfaces that support clotting reactions; fibrin makes that response more stable. The answer should connect the common pathway to the functional result rather than merely naming the final molecules.

## w08-mc26

A person with **type A blood** has A antigens on red-cell surfaces and anti-B antibodies in plasma, making **A** correct. The red-cell antigen identifies the ABO group, while the plasma antibodies help explain why incompatible red cells can be attacked.

For a red-cell transfusion, an important principle is whether the recipient's antibodies recognize antigens on the donor erythrocytes. A type A recipient's anti-B can react against B-bearing donor cells. The alternative combining B antigen with anti-B fails to describe the normal type A pattern. Type O lacks A and B antigens, but not every possible red-cell antigen, and ABO grouping does not determine Rh status. A person can be A positive or A negative. ## w08-mc27

## w08-sr01

Relate each vessel's structure to its main role. **Large arteries** have strong, elastic walls suited to receiving blood at relatively high pressure. Their expansion and recoil help smooth the intermittent output of the heart. **Arterioles** have adjustable smooth-muscle tone and small radii, making them major regulators of resistance and local tissue perfusion.

**Capillaries** have thin exchange walls and collectively provide extensive surface area. Their large combined cross-sectional area lowers average blood velocity and supports exchange. **Venules** collect blood from capillary beds and participate in exchange and inflammatory interactions in appropriate tissues. **Veins** return blood to the heart at lower pressure and are highly compliant, accommodating a substantial volume. Valves in many veins and external muscle activity help support directional return.

The answer should compare more than diameter alone. Pressure, compliance, wall composition, resistance control, exchange distance, and storage capacity explain why the vessels differ. Do not assume that a single narrow capillary makes the whole capillary network the dominant adjustable resistance site; many capillaries are arranged in parallel. Follow the circulation from pressure delivery to resistance control, exchange, collection, and return.

## w08-sr02

The expression **Q = ΔP/R** relates volume flow to the pressure difference driving it and the resistance opposing it. Increasing the upstream–downstream pressure difference increases flow if resistance is unchanged. Increasing resistance reduces flow if the gradient is unchanged. Equal absolute pressures at both ends do not create a driving gradient, even when both pressures are high.

Arteriolar radius is especially powerful because the idealized Poiseuille model gives resistance proportional to 1/r⁴ when viscosity and length are held constant. Halving radius would therefore raise resistance sixteenfold in that model; doubling it would reduce resistance to one-sixteenth. Since vascular smooth muscle can change radius, it can rapidly alter tissue perfusion and total peripheral resistance.

State the assumptions when using the numerical relationship. Real circulation includes compliant, branching vessels, changing tone, and nonideal flow conditions, so the fourth-power law is an explanatory approximation rather than an exact prediction for every intact vascular bed. Also distinguish a local change from a whole-body one. Dilation in one active tissue can increase its flow without requiring every organ's flow or systemic pressure to change in the same direction.

## w08-sr03

Linear velocity and volume flow are different quantities. Flow describes volume passing per unit time, whereas velocity describes the distance blood travels per unit time. For a given total flow, mean velocity is inversely related to the **combined cross-sectional area** of the vessels carrying that flow.

Although one capillary is extremely narrow, the systemic capillary network contains very many vessels in parallel. Their summed cross-sectional area is much larger than the area of the aorta. Blood can therefore pass through the entire network at a lower average linear velocity while maintaining the circuit's overall volume flow. The relevant comparison is not one capillary against one artery.

Slow transit and short diffusion distances are well suited to exchange of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, and wastes. However, slow velocity is not the only determinant: permeability, concentration gradients, surface area, and the number of perfused capillaries also matter. A complete answer combines the physical explanation with its physiological value. Avoid saying that narrowness alone slows capillary blood, or that low capillary velocity means the tissues receive almost no flow.

## w08-sr04

Capillary bulk fluid movement reflects the balance of forces across the wall. Capillary hydrostatic pressure tends to push fluid outward into the interstitium, while plasma colloid osmotic pressure, produced largely by proteins such as albumin, tends to retain or draw water into the vascular compartment. Interstitial hydrostatic and oncotic conditions also influence the net balance.

**Filtration** means net outward fluid movement; **absorption** means net inward movement. Do not confuse either with diffusion of an individual solute down its concentration gradient. A course diagram may show how changing pressure along a capillary can alter the predicted direction of bulk flow, but it should not be generalized into a rule that every capillary always filters at one end and reabsorbs everything at the other.

The lymphatic system returns excess filtered fluid and escaped proteins to the circulation. If filtration exceeds return capacity, plasma protein concentration falls, or lymphatic drainage is impaired, interstitial fluid can accumulate as edema. A strong answer explains the competing forces and the return pathway, not simply "water leaves and comes back." The net outcome depends on the vascular bed and physiological conditions, with lymphatics essential to sustained fluid balance.

## w08-sr05

**Mean arterial pressure** is the time-averaged arterial pressure over a cardiac cycle. It helps describe the driving pressure for systemic flow, although flow through a particular organ depends on its arterial–venous pressure difference. At ordinary resting rates, MAP is approximated by DBP + one-third of pulse pressure, where pulse pressure is SBP − DBP. This weights the estimate toward diastole, which normally lasts longer.

A complementary relationship describes its determinants: MAP − right-atrial pressure ≈ CO × TPR, commonly simplified to MAP ≈ CO × TPR. Cardiac output itself equals HR × SV. Thus heart rate, ventricular filling, contractility, afterload, and arteriolar tone can all influence MAP through their effects on flow or resistance. Blood volume influences venous return and filling rather than appearing as a separate term in the simple product.

The two equations answer different questions: one estimates MAP from a blood-pressure reading; the other explains its hemodynamic determinants. A complete answer gives both with units and qualifications. Do not equate MAP with systolic pressure alone or assume the one-third approximation is exact at every heart rate and waveform shape.

## w08-sr06

**Myogenic autoregulation** responds to mechanical stretch of vascular smooth muscle. Increased intravascular pressure stretches the wall and can trigger contraction, increasing resistance and buffering the direct flow increase. A decrease in stretch can allow relaxation. This is a local property of the vessel rather than a response that necessarily begins with a brainstem command.

**Metabolic and paracrine control** responds to tissue conditions. Increased activity consumes oxygen and produces local signals that generally promote arteriolar dilation, improving delivery and waste removal. Active hyperemia accompanies increased metabolic demand; reactive hyperemia is the increased flow following release of a temporary restriction, reflecting accumulated local influences and the preceding supply deficit.

Both mechanisms adjust local resistance, but their initiating variables differ: pressure-related stretch versus the chemical consequences of tissue activity or insufficient perfusion. Their effects can interact with sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone. Do not claim that local regulation and central blood-pressure control always produce identical responses in every organ. A well-structured comparison names the stimulus, the smooth-muscle response, the resistance change, and the functional purpose for each mechanism.

## w08-sr07

A fall in MAP reduces stretch of arterial baroreceptors in the **carotid sinus and aortic arch**. Their afferent firing decreases. Carotid sinus information travels principally through the glossopharyngeal pathway and aortic information through the vagus to medullary cardiovascular circuits. The CNS interprets the reduced input and increases sympathetic output while reducing parasympathetic influence on the heart.

At the SA node, this raises heart rate. Sympathetic beta1 effects increase ventricular contractility. Alpha1-mediated arteriolar constriction raises TPR, while venous constriction can increase venous return and support filling and stroke volume. Through CO = HR × SV and MAP ≈ CO × TPR, these changes tend to oppose the original pressure fall.

The important sign change is **less baroreceptor firing but more sympathetic output**. Do not draw those two signals as though both rise. Also, the fast reflex redistributes and changes the use of existing circulating volume; it does not instantly replace lost fluid. A complete flow chart labels the stimulus, sensors, afferent nerves, medullary integration, autonomic outputs, target tissues, and the resulting pressure correction. This remains useful written learning practice even though Lecture 22 is designated MCQ-only for the actual midterm.

## w08-sr08

Severe gastrointestinal fluid loss can reduce extracellular and circulating plasma volume. Lower circulating volume decreases the filling available to the heart: **venous return falls, EDV falls, and stroke volume tends to fall through the Frank–Starling relationship**. If compensation is insufficient, CO = HR × SV decreases and MAP ≈ CO × TPR also decreases. Reduced cerebral perfusion can then produce fainting in the tutorial scenario.

The baroreflex opposes the disturbance through increased heart rate, contractility, and vascular tone, but it may not fully compensate for a severe volume deficit. Prolonged motionless standing can worsen venous pooling and reduce the assistance provided by the skeletal-muscle pump. Use the equations to connect variables, not simply to decorate the answer. A rising HR does not guarantee normal CO if SV has fallen sufficiently. Likewise, a compensatory rise in TPR does not mean the original volume deficit has been corrected. This is a causal analysis of the supplied case, not a diagnosis of every fainting event. State the initial disturbance, the hemodynamic chain, the cerebral consequence, and the limits of compensation.

## w08-sr09

Whole blood consists of **plasma** and **formed elements**. Plasma is mostly water and contains proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, signaling molecules, and wastes. The formed elements include erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets. Red cells specialize in gas transport, white cells participate in defense, and platelets support hemostasis.

When blood is centrifuged under the relevant laboratory conditions, red cells form the major lower cellular layer, a thin buffy coat contains leukocytes and platelets, and plasma lies above. **Hematocrit** is the fraction or percentage of the total blood volume occupied by red cells. It is not the fraction occupied by all formed elements and is not the percentage of plasma made of hemoglobin.

For example, a red-cell layer occupying 45% of a sample corresponds to a hematocrit of approximately 45%. A change in hematocrit can reflect a change in red-cell volume, plasma volume, or both; it should not be interpreted in isolation as proof of a single cause. Distinguish plasma from serum as well: serum is the liquid remaining after clotting and differs in clotting-protein content. The question's central requirements are correct compartment definitions and the numerator and denominator of hematocrit.

## w08-sr10

Hematopoiesis begins with stem and progenitor cells, mainly in adult bone marrow, that produce the blood-cell lineages. Differentiation and proliferation are influenced by cytokines and growth factors. Erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelet-producing megakaryocytes therefore share developmental origins but follow distinct maturation pathways.

For red-cell production, reduced renal oxygen availability increases **EPO** signaling. EPO acts on erythroid precursors in the marrow, promoting their survival and development and increasing red-cell output. More hemoglobin-containing cells can improve oxygen-carrying capacity, reducing the original stimulus. This is a feedback loop linking tissue oxygen conditions to production of the cells that support oxygen transport.

The response takes time: it requires cell production and maturation, so it is not the seconds-scale correction provided by a baroreceptor reflex. Nutrient availability and marrow function also affect the ability to increase output. Do not locate erythrocyte production in the kidney merely because the kidney produces EPO, and do not confuse hematopoiesis with hemostasis. A complete explanation names the signal's trigger, source, target, functional result, and time scale.

## w08-sr11

Vessel injury exposes adhesive surfaces and initiates local responses that limit bleeding. Platelets adhere at the damaged region, with interactions involving exposed matrix and von Willebrand factor helping attachment. They become activated, change shape, and release mediators that recruit and activate additional platelets. Platelet–platelet interactions build the **primary platelet plug**.

Coagulation reactions then generate thrombin, which converts soluble fibrinogen to fibrin. Fibrin forms a reinforcing network, and further stabilization makes the plug more resistant to disruption. Platelets also provide surfaces supporting clotting reactions, so primary hemostasis and coagulation are interacting processes rather than completely isolated stages that never overlap.

A good answer distinguishes adhesion, activation, aggregation, and fibrin stabilization. Do not call fibrinogen and fibrin the same material, or describe thrombin as the fibrous scaffold; thrombin is the enzyme that helps produce it. Positive feedback supports rapid local amplification, while normal regulatory mechanisms help keep clotting localized. The goal is a stable seal at an injury, not indiscriminate solidification of all circulating blood. Explain how the early cellular plug and the later protein mesh complement one another.

## w08-sr12

For ABO red-cell compatibility, distinguish **antigens on erythrocytes** from **antibodies in plasma**. Type A cells carry A antigen and the person's plasma normally contains anti-B; type B shows the converse. Type AB carries both antigens, while type O lacks A and B antigens. In a red-cell transfusion, recipient antibodies reacting against donor-cell antigens can cause damaging immune reactions. Plasma compatibility follows different considerations, so do not use a red-cell rule indiscriminately for every blood product.

Rh classification commonly refers to the D antigen. An Rh-negative mother exposed to Rh-positive fetal cells may become sensitized and develop anti-D antibodies. Maternal anti-D IgG can cross the placenta and damage Rh-positive fetal erythrocytes in an affected pregnancy. Prior exposure or sensitization matters; it is not a rule that every first pregnancy is automatically protected or every later pregnancy affected.

Prophylactic anti-D immunoglobulin reduces the chance of sensitization in an appropriate unsensitized setting. It does not change the fetus's blood-group genes or represent treatment of every already established case. A complete answer links antigen recognition to red-cell injury and distinguishes ABO's usual antibody pattern from acquired anti-D sensitization. Actual compatibility testing involves additional antigens and laboratory assessment beyond this introductory model.
